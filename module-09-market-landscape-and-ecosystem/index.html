    Module 9: Market Landscape and Ecosystem¶

    9.1 Global Market Size¶

    The global Islamic finance industry has experienced exponential growth, evolving from a localized, faith-based niche into a systemic pillar of the international financial architecture. By the end of 2024, the market size was measured through two primary, widely cited lenses—each highlighting unprecedented expansion driven by strong demand, ethical appeal, and market diversification.

    Global Market Size and Growth Metrics:

    • IFSB Dataset: According to the Islamic Financial Services Board (IFSB), the core Islamic Financial Services Industry (IFSI) reached USD 3.88 trillion in total assets in 2024, recording a robust 14.9% year-on-year (YoY) growth. This growth outpaced the average rates of recent years and demonstrates broad-based expansion across Islamic banking, capital markets, and Islamic insurance (takaful).

    • LSEG/ICD Dataset: Utilizing a broader methodology that captures additional segments and emphasizes rapid commercialization, the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) and the Islamic Corporation for the Development of the Private Sector (ICD) reported global Islamic finance assets at USD 5.98 trillion in 2024, reflecting an impressive 21% YoY increase. Differences between these two figures are primarily definitional, driven by varying jurisdictional coverage, what is counted, and institutional classifications.

    The Sukuk Market Surge (Outstanding and 2024 Issuance): The Islamic capital market, driven by sukuk (Islamic bonds), is a major engine of this industry's expansion and is increasingly used for mainstream corporate and sovereign funding.

    • Outstanding Volume: Total global outstanding sukuk has surpassed the USD 900 billion mark—reaching USD 902.82 billion according to the International Islamic Financial Market (IIFM)—and successfully crossed the historic USD 1 trillion valuation threshold in 2024.

    • 2024 Issuances: The IIFM reported that total global sukuk issuances reached USD 205 billion in 2024. Governments remained the dominant issuers, utilizing sukuk to manage fiscal deficits and fund large-scale infrastructure projects.

    • International Record: Within this total, international sukuk issuances hit a record high of USD 65.6 billion, the highest level since the market's inception. This surge was fueled by improving global liquidity conditions, a decline in funding costs, and growing interest from Western and ESG-focused institutional investors looking for portfolio diversification.

    Projections to 2029: Looking ahead, the momentum of the Islamic finance industry is expected to continue aggressively. Global Islamic finance assets are projected to reach USD 9.7 trillion by 2029, growing at an estimated Compound Annual Growth Rate (CAGR) of around 10%. This monumental expansion is expected to be driven by several key factors:

    • Sovereign and Infrastructure Demand: Massive infrastructure and economic diversification requirements, particularly the GCC’s sovereign visions (such as Saudi Arabia's Vision 2030).

    • Sustainable and Green Finance: A strong alignment with Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) frameworks. The ESG sukuk market has already surpassed USD 50 billion in outstanding value, attracting secular, values-driven investors alongside traditional Islamic investors.

    • Digitalization and Fintech: The convergence of Islamic finance with digital platforms, blockchain tokenization, and cross-border connectivity, which is drastically lowering issuance costs and broadening access to retail and micro-investors.

    9.2 The Three Major Hubs¶

    The global Islamic finance industry has evolved into a sophisticated, multi-trillion-dollar ecosystem. Its growth is anchored by several key jurisdictions that play distinct, complementary roles in advancing Shariah-compliant capital markets, regulatory frameworks, and financial innovation.

    Here is an expanded look at the three major hubs and other key international players shaping the landscape of Islamic finance:

    Malaysia: ‘The Laboratory’¶

    Malaysia is widely regarded as the global leader in Islamic finance innovation, characterized by a highly developed, centralized regulatory framework.

    • Regulatory Innovation (SC/BNM): Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM) and the Securities Commission (SC) operate a highly centralized governance model. The rulings of their National Shariah Advisory Councils are binding on all Islamic financial institutions and courts in the country, creating high legal certainty and preventing "fatwa shopping".

    • IFSB Host: Kuala Lumpur is the headquarters of the Islamic Financial Services Board (IFSB), the international standard-setting body responsible for prudential regulation, capital adequacy, and corporate governance for the global Islamic financial services industry.

    • SRI Pioneer: Malaysia has pioneered the convergence of Islamic finance and Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) principles. The country issued the world’s first corporate green sukuk (Tadau Energy) in 2017. Through BNM's Value-Based Intermediation (VBI) framework, Malaysia mandates that Islamic banks integrate social and environmental impact into their core business strategies.

    Saudi Arabia: ‘The Powerhouse’¶

    Saudi Arabia represents the largest source of Islamic finance assets and banking growth globally, driven by massive state-backed economic transformation.

    • Vision 2030 and PIF: The Kingdom’s Vision 2030 aims to aggressively diversify the economy away from oil, requiring vast infrastructure and renewable energy financing. The Public Investment Fund (PIF) serves as a primary engine for this growth, issuing mega-sukuk to fund gigaprojects and strategic investments.

    • SAMA: The Saudi Central Bank (SAMA) oversees a predominantly Islamic banking sector. While historically relying on market-driven self-regulation for Shariah compliance, SAMA’s robust oversight has helped Saudi Arabia build the world's most heavily capitalized Islamic financial sector.

    • Fintech Leadership: The Kingdom boasts the largest national cluster of Islamic fintech firms globally, fueled by digital banking licenses and open banking regulations.

    United Arab Emirates: ‘The Bridge’¶

    The UAE acts as the premier global hub for cross-border structuring, international listings, and connecting GCC capital with global markets.

    • DIFC and ADGM: The Dubai International Financial Centre (DIFC) and Abu Dhabi Global Market (ADGM) operate as ‘onshore’ international financial centers governed by English common law. They provide familiar legal infrastructure for international alternative investment firms, hedge funds, and private credit platforms seeking Shariah-compliant structures.

    • Higher Shari’ah Authority (HSA): To harmonize Shariah standards and boost investor confidence, the Central Bank of the UAE established the HSA. Its fatwas and standards are binding across all Islamic financial institutions in the UAE, moving the country away from decentralized, bank-specific Shariah boards.

    • Digital Assets: The UAE is a trailblazer in digital asset regulation. Initiatives like the RAK Digital Assets Oasis and tokenization sandboxes in the DIFC/ADGM position the UAE at the forefront of Islamic DeFi, digital sukuk, and blockchain-based asset tokenization.

    Other Critical Jurisdictions¶

    The United Kingdom (The Western Hub): The UK is the leading center for Islamic finance outside the Muslim world. To create a "level playing field," the UK government systematically removed tax barriers, such as the double Stamp Duty Land Tax on Islamic mortgages. In 2014, the UK became the first Western nation to issue a sovereign sukuk. Today, the London Stock Exchange is a primary venue for dollar-denominated and green sukuk, having raised over $50 billion through dozens of issuances, and English law governs the majority of cross-border Islamic contracts globally.

    Indonesia: With a massive Muslim population, Indonesia has built an incredibly robust sovereign sukuk market. It is highly recognized for its retail sukuk program, which has successfully boosted domestic financial inclusion by allowing individual investors to participate affordably. Indonesia also issued the world's first sovereign green sukuk in 2018 and pioneered Cash Waqf-Linked Sukuk (CWLS), integrating Islamic social finance with state fiscal policy.

    Pakistan: Pakistan operates under a highly interventionist and state-driven Shariah governance model. The State Bank of Pakistan (SBP) mandates strict Shariah compliance, and following a landmark 2022 ruling by the Federal Shariat Court, the country is actively transitioning its entire financial system to be fully interest-free by 2027. Pakistan also features a unique non-bank financial sector heavily supported by specialized Mudarabah (profit-sharing) companies.

    Bahrain: Bahrain is foundational to the industry’s architecture as the host of the Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI). AAOIFI’s standards on Shariah contracts, auditing, and governance act as the definitive reference point globally and are mandatory in several Middle Eastern jurisdictions. Furthermore, the Central Bank of Bahrain issued the world’s first sovereign sukuk in 2001 and continues to provide a comprehensive, progressive regulatory rulebook.

    9.3 Key Ecosystem Players¶

    The global Islamic finance ecosystem is a sophisticated, multi-trillion-dollar architecture that integrates Shariah-compliant principles with modern capital markets. Understanding this ecosystem requires examining the specific roles of standard-setters, market facilitators, multilateral organizations, regulators, and commercial players.

    1. Shariah Standards: AAOIFI (Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions) Based in Bahrain, AAOIFI sets the fundamental "rules of the game" for Shariah compliance, accounting, auditing, ethics, and governance. With 62+ Shariah standards currently issued, AAOIFI dictates the contractual and theoretical basis for Islamic finance globally. Its standards are mandatory in jurisdictions like Bahrain, Oman, and the UAE, and serve as highly influential guidelines in others. AAOIFI’s recent initiatives—such as Standard 59 on the Sale of Debt and the highly debated Standard 62 on Sukuk—profoundly shape market practice by mandating strict asset-tangibility ratios and addressing the industry tension between legal form and economic substance.

    2. Prudential Standards: IFSB (Islamic Financial Services Board) While AAOIFI governs Shariah rules, the Malaysia-based IFSB focuses on the prudential stability of the industry. The IFSB adapts global regulatory frameworks, such as the Basel III accords, to fit the specific risk profiles of Islamic institutions. It issues binding guidance on capital adequacy, risk management, corporate governance, and the treatment of unique Islamic finance risks, such as "Displaced Commercial Risk" and "Equity Investment Risk". Furthermore, the IFSB acts as the primary data tracker for the industry’s health through its annual Islamic Financial Services Industry (IFSI) Stability Report.

    3. Market Documentation: IIFM (International Islamic Financial Market) The Bahrain-based IIFM focuses on standardizing the legal documentation for Islamic treasury, hedging, and sukuk markets to reduce transaction costs and legal uncertainty. Bilateral, bespoke agreements historically hindered Islamic liquidity; the IIFM resolves this by creating unified frameworks. Key breakthroughs include the ISDA/IIFM Tahawwut Master Agreement for Shariah-compliant hedging (profit rate and cross-currency swaps) and the Master Collateralized Murabaha Agreement (MCMA). Recently, IIFM partnered with the International Capital Market Association (ICMA) to develop a standardized Islamic Repo (I’aadat Al Shira’a) Master Agreement, which aims to unlock trapped liquidity by establishing an efficient short-term funding market akin to the conventional Global Master Repurchase Agreement (GMRA).

    4. Liquidity Instruments: IILM (International Islamic Liquidity Management Corporation) A structural bottleneck for Islamic banks is the prohibition of interest-bearing conventional treasury bills, which limits their access to High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA). Based in Kuala Lumpur, the IILM was established by central banks and multilateral organizations to bridge this cross-border liquidity gap. It issues highly rated (A-1/F1), short-term Shariah-compliant sukuk (with maturities up to 364 days) backed by sovereign and supranational assets. By regularly auctioning these instruments, the IILM provides a vital risk-free yield curve and enables Islamic financial institutions to manage their balance sheets efficiently.

    5. Global Multilaterals: IsDB, IMF, and World Bank Multilateral institutions provide development finance, policy framing, and capacity building.

    • Islamic Development Bank (IsDB): Acting as a "Lender of Last Resort" and primary market developer, the IsDB pioneers Islamic Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs), establishes industry infrastructure, and collaborates on sustainable/green sukuk frameworks.

    • World Bank: Through entities like the IFC and MIGA, the World Bank actively invests in Islamic syndicated loans, provides political risk insurance for Islamic project finance, and issues its own sukuk. Its Global Islamic Finance Development Center in Istanbul provides critical advisory services to emerging markets.

    • IMF (International Monetary Fund): The IMF integrates Islamic finance into its macroeconomic surveillance, Financial Sector Assessment Programs (FSAPs), and technical assistance, ensuring the sector's systemic stability.

    6. Key Regulators: BNM, SC Malaysia, CBUAE, and SAMA National regulators define local Shariah governance and shape capital market competitiveness.

    • Malaysia (BNM & SC Malaysia): Bank Negara Malaysia and the Securities Commission operate a highly centralized model. They utilize a National Shariah Advisory Council (SAC) whose rulings are binding on all institutions and courts, ensuring high legal certainty and preventing "fatwa shopping".

    • UAE (CBUAE): The Central Bank of the UAE has transitioned to a centralized model by establishing the Higher Shari’ah Authority (HSA). The HSA issues binding standards and approves liquidity management instruments, standardizing practices across the UAE’s $195.4 billion Islamic banking sector.

    • Saudi Arabia (SAMA): The Saudi Central Bank oversees the world's largest single market for Islamic banking, where Islamic assets comprise 75–80% of the total banking sector. SAMA enforces comprehensive regulatory and Shariah governance frameworks to support the massive asset growth fueled by Vision 2030.

    7. Islamic Banks: Al Rajhi, Dubai Islamic Bank, and KFH Islamic banking remains the dominant sector, holding approximately 72% of total global Islamic finance assets. Giants like Al Rajhi Bank (Saudi Arabia), Dubai Islamic Bank (the UAE-based pioneer established in 1975), and Kuwait Finance House (KFH) drive large-scale retail and corporate financing. They utilize contracts like Murabaha (cost-plus financing), Ijara (leasing), and Musharaka (partnerships) to provide functionally competitive alternatives to conventional lending.

    8. Fintechs: Digitalization and Democratization The ecosystem is being rapidly disrupted by over 300 Islamic fintechs globally, with Saudi Arabia hosting the largest cluster of 74 firms. These platforms address structural inefficiencies by driving digital sukuk issuance, AI-driven compliance, and crowdfunding. Blockchain platforms (e.g., Wethaq, ADIB’s Smart Sukuk) are fractionalizing asset ownership, drastically reducing the traditional USD 500,000–$1.5 million issuance costs, and enabling SMEs to issue "micro-sukuk". Additionally, firms like Finterra are revolutionizing Islamic social finance by placing Waqf (endowments) and Zakat on the blockchain for transparent management.

    9. Indices: Gatekeepers of Islamic Equity (Dow Jones, FTSE, MSCI) Covering an estimated USD 4.3 trillion market, Islamic indices such as the Dow Jones Islamic Market Index, FTSE Shariah, and MSCI Islamic function as the gatekeepers for Shariah-compliant public equity. They employ rigorous two-tier screening methodologies:

    1. Business Activity Screens: Excluding companies involved in prohibited (haram) sectors like alcohol, gambling, pork production, and conventional interest-based financial services.

    2. Financial Ratio Screens: Applying strict thresholds to debt-to-equity ratios and interest-bearing liquidity to ensure a company is not overly leveraged or reliant on conventional interest income.

    Executive Refresher¶

    • Size and Growth Context: The global Islamic finance industry exceeded USD 4 trillion in total assets as of the latest IFSB Stability Report, with Islamic banking comprising approximately 70% of assets, sukuk markets around 20%, and Takaful and Islamic funds making up the remainder. Understanding this composition helps practitioners calibrate opportunity size across product lines and geographies when advising clients on Islamic finance strategy.

    • Three Core Jurisdiction Clusters: The Islamic finance market is dominated by three clusters: (1) the GCC, where Islamic banking is deeply embedded in retail and corporate banking; (2) Southeast Asia (Malaysia, Indonesia, Brunei), where the most sophisticated regulatory and capital market infrastructure exists; and (3) South Asia (Pakistan, Bangladesh), where Islamic banking is growing rapidly from a smaller base. Each cluster has distinct Shariah standards, regulatory approaches, and product preferences.

    • Non-Muslim Investor Base is Significant: Approximately 20-30% of sukuk investors in international issuances are conventional (non-Islamic) investors seeking portfolio diversification, yield pickup over equivalent conventional bonds, and exposure to GCC/Asian credit risk. Practitioners should not assume Islamic finance markets are exclusively Muslim -- structuring and disclosure decisions must consider the preferences of both Shariah-constrained and conventional investors simultaneously.

    • Infrastructure Gaps Remain Commercial Risks and Opportunities: Despite the industry's growth, key gaps persist: shallow secondary sukuk markets with wide bid-offer spreads, limited availability of Basel III-qualifying HQLAs for Islamic banks, inconsistency of Shariah standards across jurisdictions, and a shortage of AAOIFI-certified professionals. These gaps represent both risk factors for existing participants and commercial opportunities for infrastructure builders.

    • Digital and Fintech Transformation is Accelerating: Islamic fintech is growing rapidly in Malaysia, UAE, Indonesia, and the UK. Key verticals include Islamic digital banks, peer-to-peer Islamic financing platforms, halal robo-advisory services, and tokenised sukuk. Central banks in Malaysia, UAE, and Bahrain have established regulatory sandboxes specifically accommodating Islamic fintech within existing licensing regimes.

    A) Exceed USD 4 trillion, with Islamic banking comprising approximately 70% of total industry assets

    B) Exceed USD 1 trillion, concentrated primarily in Malaysia and Indonesia as the largest Islamic finance hubs

    C) Exceed USD 10 trillion, with the GCC accounting for over 60% of total Islamic financial assets globally

    D) Remain below USD 500 billion, mainly in the form of outstanding sukuk issuances in international markets

    2. Malaysia's position as the world's most developed Islamic capital market is underpinned by:

    A) Having the largest Islamic bank by total assets (Maybank Islamic) among all global Islamic financial institutions

    B) A comprehensive dual banking system, the largest sukuk market by issuance volume, dedicated Islamic capital market legislation, and a centralised binding Shariah Advisory Council

    C) Mandatory Islamic finance requirements for all new financial products issued in the domestic market

    D) The highest number of AAOIFI-certified scholars sitting on Malaysian Shariah supervisory boards globally

    3. The GCC's Islamic finance growth since 2008 has been primarily driven by:

    A) Mandatory conversion of all conventional banks to full Islamic banking operations by government decree

    B) Growing demand from retail depositors for Shariah-compliant savings products in lieu of conventional accounts

    C) Declining global oil prices that forced GCC sovereigns to issue sukuk rather than draw on sovereign reserves

    D) Government-led initiatives, oil wealth recycling into Islamic banking, and regulatory frameworks mandating Islamic windows or fully Islamic banking licences for new entrants

    4. Non-Muslim investor participation in international sukuk markets is primarily driven by:

    A) Religious arbitrage opportunities unavailable in conventional fixed income and credit markets

    B) Regulatory requirements in certain jurisdictions for asset managers to hold a minimum allocation to Islamic instruments

    C) The diversification benefits and often comparable or superior risk-adjusted returns of investment-grade sukuk relative to conventional bonds of equivalent credit quality from the same issuer

    D) Tax incentives specifically available to non-Muslim investors in GCC sukuk issuances under bilateral treaty arrangements

    5. The role of the IILM (International Islamic Liquidity Management Corporation) in the Islamic finance ecosystem is to:

    A) Provide Shariah-compliant export credit guarantees for Islamic trade finance transactions globally

    B) Issue short-term high-quality liquid sukuk instruments that enable Islamic banks to meet Basel III LCR requirements without holding conventional government bonds

    C) Manage the Shariah scholars' certification database and coordinate SSB appointments across IILM member jurisdictions

    D) Operate the global Islamic interbank foreign exchange market as a neutral central counterparty for FX transactions

    Answer Key¶

    Q Answer Explanation
    1 A The IFSB Stability Report estimates total Islamic finance assets at over USD 4 trillion, with Islamic banking the dominant segment at ~70%, followed by sukuk (~20%), and Islamic funds/Takaful.
    2 B Malaysia combines a dual banking system, the world's largest sukuk market by cumulative issuance, the Islamic Financial Services Act (2013), and a legally binding centralised SAC — the most comprehensive ecosystem globally.
    3 D GCC Islamic banking growth was state-driven — through Vision 2030-type initiatives, sovereign sukuk programmes, and regulatory frameworks requiring Islamic banking presence from new market entrants.
    4 C Non-Muslim investors participate because investment-grade sukuk (particularly sovereign and quasi-sovereign) offer diversification and comparable yields to equivalent conventional bonds — commercial logic, not religious motivation.
    5 B IILM's mandate is to issue short-term investment-grade sukuk qualifying as Basel III HQLAs — directly addressing the liquidity management gap for Islamic banks that cannot hold conventional government securities.
