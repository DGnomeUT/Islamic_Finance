# Module 8: Case Study Compendium


## 8.1 UK Sovereign Sukuk (Ijara, 2014)

In June 2014, the United Kingdom made a landmark entry into the global Islamic capital markets by issuing a **GBP 200 million** sovereign sukuk [1-3]. This issuance marked a major milestone as the UK became the **first Western country outside the Islamic world** to issue a sovereign Islamic bond [1, 2, 4, 5]. The 5-year sukuk was a resounding success, attracting over £2 billion in orders from global investors and closing 10 times oversubscribed [1, 2]. 

**Key Parties Involved**
The transaction was structured to integrate seamlessly into the UK's governmental and financial architecture:
*   **HM Treasury:** Acted as the transaction sponsor and ultimately provided a Procurement Undertaking (via a Deed of Covenant) granting investors direct recourse to the government, ensuring the sukuk carried the same sovereign credit backing as a conventional UK Gilt [4, 6-8].
*   **The SPV:** A dedicated Special Purpose Vehicle, **HM Treasury UK Sovereign Sukuk PLC**, was incorporated to act as the issuer and trustee [9, 10]. The SPV was wholly owned by HM Treasury [9]. 
*   **Lead Managers & Structuring Banks:** **HSBC** served as the structuring adviser and a joint lead manager [11, 12]. **Standard Chartered**, along with Barwa Bank, CIMB, and the National Bank of Abu Dhabi, also served as joint lead managers for the issuance [11-13]. 

**The Ijara Structure and Government Property Assets**
To comply with Islamic prohibitions against interest (riba), the UK utilized a **sukuk al-ijarah** (a lease-based structure), which is highly favored globally for its straightforward lease/rent economics [1, 14-16]. 
*   The underlying assets consisted of **government property**, specifically plots of land and buildings used for administrative and governmental purposes by the Secretary of State for Communities and Local Government (DCLG) [17-19]. 
*   Using the £200 million raised from investors, the SPV acquired a 99-year head lease over these properties from the DCLG [18-20].
*   The SPV then sub-leased the properties back to the DCLG [18, 21]. 
*   In return, the DCLG made periodic rental payments to the SPV, which were passed on to the investors as a profit distribution of 2.036% per annum [3, 18, 21, 22]. 
*   At maturity in 2019, the SPV had the right to require the DCLG to purchase back the unexpired term of the lease, returning the principal distribution to the certificate holders [23].

**Tax Neutrality Legislation**
A critical enabler of this issuance was the UK government's proactive legislative reform designed to create a "level playing field" between Islamic and conventional finance [24, 25]. 
*   **The Finance Act 2008 and GAFAR 2014:** The Finance Act 2008 established the legal concept of "alternative finance arrangements," granting HM Treasury the mandate to raise money without paying interest [26, 27]. This was later actualized by the Government Alternative Finance Arrangements Regulations (GAFAR) of 2014, which explicitly authorized the Treasury to transfer land interests to an SPV and enter into leaseback agreements [26-28].
*   **Stamp Duty Exemption:** A typical Ijara structure involves multiple property transfers, which would traditionally trigger double Stamp Duty Land Tax (SDLT), making the transaction prohibitively expensive [29, 30]. Schedule 61 of the Finance Act 2009 removed these tax barriers, exempting the sukuk from SDLT and ensuring it was treated identically to a conventional UK Gilt for tax purposes [31-33]. 

**Lessons Learned**
The UK's debut sovereign sukuk provided essential insights for the global Islamic finance industry:
*   **Legal and Tax Infrastructure is Paramount:** A successful sovereign sukuk relies heavily on clean documentation and tax/legal neutrality, not just Shariah design [34, 35]. The UK proved that a robust Islamic finance ecosystem can be successfully engineered in a non-Muslim majority country through careful legislative adaptation [36].
*   **Asset-Based Recourse:** While the investors held beneficial ownership of the government properties, the commercial reality was that the sukuk was an "asset-based" instrument. In the event of a default, investors ultimately relied on the sovereign creditworthiness of the UK government rather than liquidating the underlying physical real estate [8, 15, 35]. 
*   **Balancing Purity and Practicality:** While stricter "asset-backed" structures offer deeper Shariah authenticity by transferring actual market risk to investors, they drastically increase complexity and cost [15, 34]. The UK's strategic choice to use a familiar, asset-based Ijara structure perfectly balanced Shariah compliance with the operational simplicity required to attract a massive global investor base [14, 15, 34].


## 8.2 UAE Treasury Sukuk Programme

The **UAE Treasury Sukuk (T-Sukuk) Programme** is a landmark initiative established by the UAE Federal Government, acting through the Ministry of Finance, with the Central Bank of the UAE (CBUAE) acting as the issuing and payment agent [1, 2]. The programme represents a highly sophisticated approach to sovereign Islamic finance, balancing strict Shariah compliance with the strategic economic goals of the state.

**Multi-Contract Structure**
To match sovereign funding needs with Shariah constraints, the T-Sukuk programme utilizes a complex **multi-contract hybrid structure** [3, 4]. The UAE Federal Government Sukuk Programme Ltd acts as the issuance Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV), serving simultaneously as the issuer, trustee, and lessor [5, 6]. The issuance proceeds are allocated across two core transactional components to maintain compliance:
*   **Ijara Component (Usufruct Rights):** To satisfy the Shariah requirement for asset backing, a minimum of 50% of the proceeds (maintaining a Tangible Asset Ratio of more than 50%) is used to purchase 50-year usufruct rights over tangible, commercially leasable government infrastructure, such as roads, highways, bridges, and dams [7]. These assets are subsequently leased back to the government [5].
*   **Murabaha Component:** The remaining proceeds are deployed under a Master Murabaha Agreement to purchase commodities that are sold to the government on a deferred payment basis [8]. 

This core framework is supported by a web of supplementary documents, including servicing agency agreements, purchase and substitution undertakings, and total loss event payment undertakings [5, 9]. While this multi-contract approach provides the flexibility to blend tangible assets with debt receivables, it inherently introduces higher operational and legal execution risks [3, 4].

**Programme Format for Repeat Issuance**
Rather than executing standalone issuances, the UAE structured the T-Sukuk as an overarching issuance programme, which allows for highly efficient, multi-tranche repeat auctions [3, 4]. For example, the October 2025 auction successfully issued AED 1.1 billion across multiple tenors, attracting bids worth AED 4.57 billion (a 4.2x oversubscription) from primary dealers [2, 10]. 

This programme format achieves several vital commercial and macroeconomic objectives:
*   **Building a Benchmark Yield Curve:** Regular issuance across different maturities (e.g., 2-year, 3-year, and 5-year tenors) establishes a reliable Dirham-denominated yield curve, which serves as a vital pricing reference for future private sector corporate issuances [4, 11, 12].
*   **Market Deepening and Liquidity:** It diversifies the government's financing sources, supports the CBUAE in managing systemic liquidity, and expands the investor base to include local, international, and retail participants [3, 11-13].

**Lessons on Centralised Governance: The Role of the HSA**
The UAE Treasury Sukuk Programme serves as a prime case study in the benefits of **centralised Shariah governance**. The programme's structure, mechanism, and documentation were formally reviewed and approved via a Fatwa issued by the Higher Shari'ah Authority (HSA) at the CBUAE [1, 6, 9, 14]. The HSA acts as the supreme and binding Shariah reference for the entire UAE financial sector [15, 16].

The implementation of this centralised model offers several critical lessons for the global Islamic finance industry:
*   **Enhanced Standardisation and Legal Certainty:** By relying on a central national authority rather than individual, institution-level Shariah boards, the UAE effectively eliminates "fatwa shopping" and the interpretive divergence that often plagues decentralized markets [17-19]. This top-down standardisation harmonizes rulings and provides a higher degree of legal certainty for investors [13, 20, 21].
*   **Boosted Market Confidence:** A unified sovereign Fatwa mitigates systemic governance risk, giving institutional and retail investors absolute confidence that the instruments are authentically Shariah-compliant and will not face sudden religious challenges (such as those seen in historical corporate Sukuk defaults) [13, 22, 23].
*   **The Trade-off with Innovation:** The primary lesson and inherent trade-off of a strictly centralised governance model is that while it vastly improves standardisation and market trust, it can sometimes reduce the flexibility needed for rapid, bespoke product innovation [13, 22, 24].


## 8.3 Saudi PIF International Sukuk Programme

The Saudi Public Investment Fund (PIF) International Sukuk Programme exemplifies how major sovereign wealth funds can efficiently leverage Islamic capital markets to support massive infrastructure and strategic investment demands, such as those accelerating the Saudi Vision 2030 economic transformation [1, 2]. PIF has become a dominant issuer in the market, executing multi-billion dollar capital market and syndicated deals to meet its expansive funding requirements [1, 3, 4]. 

**Multi-Currency Approach and Programme Flexibility**
The PIF executes its capital raising through a **multi-currency programme approach**, formalized via a comprehensive offering circular [5-7]. Rather than constructing a completely new legal and Shariah structure from scratch for every capital raise, this programme-level framework allows the specific details and underlying asset structures to vary by series [5, 6]. It relies on recognized sukuk contractual templates and undertakings that can be adapted as needed [5, 6]. 

The primary benefit of this **programme flexibility is that it drastically improves issuance speed**, allowing the fund to rapidly tap into liquidity when market conditions are favorable [8, 9]. Despite this streamlined approach, the flexibility introduces a specific risk profile: investors must still conduct careful due diligence on the asset composition and contractual undertakings at the individual series level, which is especially critical as global sukuk standards and Shariah expectations continue to evolve [8, 9].

**LSE ISM Listing**
To maximize its global reach, the PIF programme targets listing its securities on the **London Stock Exchange’s International Securities Market (LSE ISM)** [5, 6]. Utilizing the LSE—a globally recognized financial hub that has established itself as a premier venue for dollar-denominated sukuk—grants the PIF direct access to a deep, diversified pool of international institutional investors who might otherwise be inaccessible in localized markets [5, 10, 11]. 

**Lessons on Efficiency for Sovereign-Backed Issuers**
The PIF's strategy provides a critical **lesson in operational efficiency for repeat, sovereign-backed issuers with strong credit profiles**: upfront programme documentation is a powerful enabler [9]. By establishing a flexible, multi-currency issuance umbrella, sovereign entities can bypass the repetitive, time-consuming, and costly legal and administrative processes associated with standalone issuances [9]. 

Furthermore, beyond internal efficiency, these robust, sovereign-backed programmes play a vital macroeconomic role. By issuing standard, high-profile paper into the international markets, **they establish essential pricing and structural benchmarks** that guide and stabilize the rest of the Islamic capital market [9].


## 8.4 ADNOC Murban Debut Sukuk (2025)

**ADNOC Murban Debut Sukuk Overview**
In 2025, the Abu Dhabi National Oil Company (ADNOC) successfully raised USD 1.5 billion through its debut 10-year "Murban" sukuk [1-3]. Issued under a newly established international sukuk program, the transaction was spearheaded by Standard Chartered, which acted as the sole global coordinator for the corporate group [1, 4, 5]. 

**Strategic Rationale and Investor Demand**
The primary commercial rationale behind the ADNOC Murban sukuk was the diversification of the company's funding sources [5]. By utilizing a Shariah-compliant structure, ADNOC was able to effectively tap into a dedicated pool of Islamic investor demand while simultaneously attracting conventional market investors, maximizing their reach across global capital markets [5].

**Lessons on Corporate Sukuk as a Bond Complement**
This landmark issuance provides several key lessons regarding the evolving role of corporate sukuk:
*   **Mainstream Corporate Adoption:** The transaction reflects the growing trend of major corporate entities adopting sukuk as a mainstream, core funding tool rather than a niche product [1, 3].
*   **A Strategic Complement to Conventional Bonds:** The ADNOC deal demonstrates that major corporates increasingly view sukuk issuance as a direct complement to their conventional bond programs, allowing them to optimize their capital structure and access broader, differentiated liquidity pools [5]. 
*   **Execution and Risk Considerations:** While the issuance was a market execution success, it highlights ongoing structural considerations for corporate issuers. For sukuk to function effectively alongside conventional debt, issuers must ensure that investors clearly understand the program's structure, the underlying asset linkage, and the credit reliance [5]. Additionally, maintaining and managing secondary trading liquidity remains a key risk factor for such international corporate sukuk programs [5].


## 8.5 Tadau Energy Green Sukuk (Malaysia, 2017)

In 2017, Malaysia's Tadau Energy Sdn Bhd made history by issuing the **world's first Green Sukuk** [1], [2], [3]. 

**Project Details and Financials**
The landmark issuance raised **MYR 250 million (approximately USD 59 million)** to fund the development of a **50-megawatt (MW) large-scale solar photovoltaic power plant** in Sabah, Malaysia [2], [4], [3]. The offering was **oversubscribed**, demonstrating immense market appetite and proving that "green" and "Islamic" instruments represent a highly attractive, unified asset class for global investors [3], [5].

**Islamic Finance Structure**
To meet both environmental and religious standards, the transaction was structured using a **Wakala (agency) structure with commodity Murabaha tranches** [4], [3]. The Shariah-compliant foundation of the deal was backed by tangible, project-specific underlying assets—namely, the **solar panels and the power purchase agreements (PPAs)** [4], [3]. 

**Market Impact: Spurring Quantum Solar**
Tadau Energy's pioneering success proved the commercial viability of green Islamic finance and acted as a direct catalyst for larger projects [3], [5]. Following Tadau's debut, **Quantum Solar Park** launched a massive follow-on program, issuing a **MYR 1 billion to 1.25 billion green sukuk** to construct three additional large-scale solar plants across Malaysia [1], [6], [2], [3], [5]. 

**The Malaysia SRI Framework Lesson**
A crucial takeaway from the Tadau Energy issuance is the importance of a supportive regulatory environment [3], [5]. The sukuk was issued under the Securities Commission Malaysia's **Sustainable and Responsible Investment (SRI) Sukuk framework** [2], [3]. The primary lesson is that **clear regulatory incentives drive sustainable issuance**, while official endorsements—such as the approval from the Shariah Advisory Council (SAC)—provide the necessary market confidence to attract a diverse, global investor base [3], [5].


## 8.6 Republic of Indonesia Sovereign Green Sukuk (2018)

In March 2018, Indonesia made history by issuing the world's first sovereign green sukuk, raising USD 1.25 billion over a five-year tenor [1-4]. This landmark transaction was developed and implemented within a remarkably short two-month window through a close partnership with the World Bank [1, 2]. **The World Bank's involvement was instrumental in building the local green sukuk market**, which included forming a Technical Working Group with Indonesia's Central Bank and Securities Commission, sharing international best practices, and providing guidance on eligible projects and third-party reviews [5].

**The Wakala Structure and Underlying Assets**
The USD 1.25 billion issuance utilized a *Wakala* (agency) structure [3, 4, 6]. To underpin the sukuk, the transaction uniquely pooled state-owned assets—specifically land and buildings, which accounted for 51% of the pool—alongside project assets that were either under construction or planned, which made up the remaining 49% [1, 2]. Because this was an asset-based sovereign deal, the ultimate credit risk remained with the Indonesian government rather than the specific assets themselves [3, 4]. 

**Use of Proceeds: Rail and Renewable Energy**
The net proceeds from the sukuk were strictly earmarked to finance and refinance eligible environmentally friendly projects aligned with Indonesia's Green Bond and Green Sukuk Framework [1, 2]. **The capital was directed toward critical sustainable infrastructure**, including the construction of over 690 kilometers of railway tracks to bolster sustainable transportation, and an increase of 7.3 million kWh in renewable electricity capacity [7, 8]. The funds also supported improvements in solid waste management for more than 7 million households [7, 8]. Collectively, these initiatives are projected to reduce greenhouse gas emissions by up to 10.3 million tons of CO2 equivalent [7, 8].

**Market Reception and 29% New Investors**
The offering was a massive market success, achieving an oversubscription rate of 2.4 times [9, 10]. Crucially, the "green" label succeeded in attracting a broad, global investor base that extended far beyond traditional Islamic finance participants. **Approximately 29% of the buyers were entirely new green investors who had never previously purchased Islamic paper** [3, 4, 9, 10]. The final distribution was highly diversified, highlighting the global appeal of the instrument: 32% of the allocation went to Islamic investors in the Middle East and Malaysia, 25% to other Asian investors, 18% to US investors, 15% to European investors, and 10% to domestic Indonesian investors [9, 10].

**The Green/Islamic Convergence Lesson**
This transaction serves as a powerful demonstration of the **profound "natural synergy" between Islamic finance principles and global Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) investing** [11, 12]. Both financial frameworks share foundational similarities, including a mandate for real-economy linkage, transparent use of proceeds, ethical stewardship of the earth, and strict negative screening that avoids harmful industries [12-14]. 

The overarching lesson from the Indonesian Sovereign Green Sukuk is that "Green" and "Islamic" labels represent highly compatible asset classes that, when combined, act as a powerful "liquidity multiplier" [3, 4, 15]. By bridging these two worlds, issuers can break out of traditional regional silos to access a massive global pool of capital, appealing simultaneously to Shariah-conscious investors and secular, ESG-focused institutional allocators [3, 4, 14].


## 8.7 Majid Al Futtaim Corporate Green Sukuk (UAE, 2019)

**Majid Al Futtaim (MAF)** made history in May 2019 by launching the **world’s first benchmark corporate green sukuk**, which was also the first green capital markets offering by a corporate entity in the Middle East and North Africa (MENA) region [1-13]. 

**Financial Details and Structure**
The inaugural green sukuk successfully raised **USD 600 million** with a 10-year tenor and a coupon rate of 4.637%, maturing in May 2029 [2, 4, 12, 14, 15]. To ensure Shariah compliance, the instrument was structured as a **Wakala** (operating as a *Wakala/Murabaha* hybrid) [6, 7, 15]. This structure allowed MAF to use its real estate portfolio as the underlying asset base to generate returns for investors [6]. The issuance was highly successful and heavily oversubscribed by a geographically diverse base of investors across Asia, Europe, and the MENA region, demonstrating strong international confidence in both the issuer and the green sukuk asset class [14, 16]. 

**Use of Proceeds and Green Buildings in Dubai and Egypt**
The funds raised were strictly earmarked to finance and refinance MAF's existing and future green projects, focusing heavily on **certified green buildings**, renewable energy, energy efficiency, and sustainable water management [3, 5, 14, 15, 17]. 

The underlying assets backing the sukuk were comprised of prominent sustainable commercial properties, specifically **hotels, offices, and shopping malls located in Dubai (UAE) and Egypt** [3, 5-7]. Key properties in the green sukuk portfolio included:
*   **Dubai, UAE:** LEED-certified buildings such as the Aloft Hotel, Hilton Garden Inn, Sheraton Mall of the Emirates, Ibis Al Rigga, Pullman Hotel, City Centre Mirdif, and the Majid Al Futtaim Towers [3, 5, 18-26].
*   **Cairo, Egypt:** Large-scale retail destinations including City Centre Almaza and Mall of Egypt, both of which hold LEED Gold certifications for Building Design and Construction [3, 5, 27-30].

**Strategic Impact**
This landmark USD 600 million issuance was a crucial milestone that shifted the MENA green sukuk market from being sovereign-dominated to including major private sector corporate issuances [6, 7]. It directly supported MAF’s ambitious "Net Positive" sustainability strategy, which targets reducing the company's carbon emissions and water consumption to a point where it puts more back into the environment than it extracts by the year 2040 [2-5, 31].


## 8.8 Malaysia SRI Sukuk Ecosystem

**Malaysia's Securities Commission (SC) Leadership in the SRI Ecosystem**
Malaysia has positioned itself as the global pioneer at the intersection of Islamic finance and sustainable investing, driven largely by the proactive leadership of the Securities Commission Malaysia (SC) [1]. The SC laid the foundation for this ecosystem by introducing the Sustainable and Responsible Investment (SRI) Sukuk Framework in 2014 [2, 3]. Continuing its regulatory leadership, the SC established explicit, forward-looking requirements for SRI sukuk, SRI-linked sukuk, and ASEAN sustainability-linked sukuk [4, 5]. 

Under the SC's Lodge and Launch Framework, the SRI guidelines mandate strict adherence to four core components: the exclusive utilization of proceeds for eligible green, social, or waqf projects; a clear process for project evaluation; the segregated management of proceeds; and comprehensive annual reporting to investors via a designated website [6-8]. Aided by this highly supportive regulatory architecture, Malaysia's SRI sukuk issuances surged ninefold to RM11.9 billion in 2024 [9].

**Cagamas’ First ASEAN Social SRI Sukuk**
A landmark transaction showcasing the practical success of this ecosystem is the issuance by Cagamas Berhad of Malaysia's first ASEAN Social SRI Sukuk [10, 11]. Structured as variable rate notes, this issuance successfully embedded Islamic finance mechanics into sustainability-linked objectives [10, 11]. This transaction demonstrated how sustainable sukuk frameworks can be utilized to broaden an issuer's investor base—capturing ESG-sensitive institutional capital—while remaining strictly within Shariah-compliant issuance parameters [12].

**KPI and Reporting Credibility Risks**
Despite the rapid growth of the market, sustainable Islamic finance instruments face unique operational and reputational challenges. The most prominent of these are **KPI and reporting credibility risks**, which are especially acute in sustainability-linked structures [11, 12]. In an SRI-linked sukuk, the financial or structural characteristics of the instrument (such as the profit rate payable to investors) vary depending on whether the issuer achieves predefined Sustainability Performance Targets (SPTs) tied to specific Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) [13-15]. 

If an issuer's impact reporting, use-of-proceeds tracking, or compliance disclosures are weak, the integrity of the instrument is compromised [11, 12]. This exposes issuers to a dual reputational hazard: **"greenwashing"** (misrepresenting or exaggerating the environmental/social impact) and **"Shariah-washing"** (failing to uphold the substantive ethical and structural requirements of Islamic jurisprudence) [11, 12, 16]. To mitigate these risks, regulations require that selected KPIs be highly strategic, measurable, externally verifiable, and subject to independent external reviews both pre- and post-issuance [17-19].

**The Lesson of Regulatory Clarity**
The defining lesson from the evolution of Malaysia’s SRI sukuk market is that **regulatory clarity serves as a major competitive advantage** [11, 12]. By providing explicit, codified guidelines and recognizing broader ASEAN standards, the SC has made product pipelines much easier to scale, structure, and audit [11, 12]. Standardized impact reporting and clear Shariah governance frameworks significantly reduce the due diligence friction for international allocators, thereby attracting global liquidity and cementing Malaysia’s role as the benchmark for sustainable Islamic capital markets [11, 20].


## 8.9 Emirates Islamic EmFIN Platform

The **Emirates Islamic EmFIN (ENBD Shari’ah Private Financing Fund SP)** is an innovative private financing platform managed by Emirates NBD Asset Management that allows professional investors to access global private credit markets while maintaining strict adherence to Islamic financial principles [1-3]. 

**Shariah-Compliant Private Financing Platform**
EmFIN operates as a Shariah-compliant fund wrapper that provides exposure to private market deal flows, specifically targeting senior secured facilities [4-6]. To remain Shariah-compliant while mirroring the performance of conventional private credit, EmFIN does not invest directly into conventional debt [7]. Instead, the fund invests in a curated Portfolio of Shariah-Compliant Assets (SCP) under the oversight of Emirates NBD’s Internal Shari’ah Supervisory Committee (ISSC) [7, 8]. The returns of these Shariah-compliant investments are then benchmarked to the performance of a specific Reference Asset—the BlackRock Private Credit Fund iCapital Offshore Access Fund, L.P. [8, 9]. 

**Wakala Charges**
The fund utilizes a *Wakala* (agency) structure, appointing Emirates NBD Asset Management as the *Wakeel* (Asset Manager) to manage the investments on behalf of the investors [8]. To cover the costs of managing the Shariah-compliant portfolio and maintaining the compliance layers, the structure incorporates specific Wakala charges, which are set at 0.20% on the fund's net assets [4, 9]. 

**Digital Onboarding and Evergreen Features**
Commercially, EmFIN was designed to democratize private asset investing for local investors by utilizing modern conveniences such as digital onboarding [5, 10]. A major selling point of the platform is its **evergreen, continuously offered structure** [7, 11]. Unlike traditional private market funds that trap capital through strict lock-up periods and complex capital calls, EmFIN's evergreen framework offers an investor-friendly model that allows for ongoing subscriptions and provides periodic (quarterly) liquidity [3, 7]. 

**Governance Risks**
Using an Islamic wrapper to reference conventional private credit introduces distinct Shariah and governance risks [5, 10]. 
* **Stress-Testing and Asset Composition:** The structure must be meticulously stress-tested to ensure that the economic exposure and permissible asset composition do not violate Islamic laws, as "Islamic private credit" can be structured in numerous ways [5, 10].
* **Interpretive Divergence:** A major governance risk is that different Shariah boards may disagree on the acceptability of benchmarking a Shariah fund to a conventional private credit asset [5]. 
* **Transparency Demands:** Because the fund is benchmarked to BlackRock's conventional assets, investors and regulators increasingly demand absolute transparency regarding where the actual cash flows are sourced to ensure profits are generated ethically [10]. 
* **Defensibility:** To protect the fund from reputational damage, the platform must maintain a highly defensible governance model with clear, series-specific disclosures, rigorous internal Shariah review processes, and unambiguous fatwas [12].


## 8.10 Dana Gas Sukuk Restructuring (2017-2018)

The Dana Gas restructuring represents a watershed moment in Islamic finance, centered around a **USD 700 million corporate Mudaraba sukuk** issued by the UAE-based natural gas company Dana Gas PJSC [1, 2]. 

In 2017, **Dana Gas shocked the global market by declaring its own sukuk unlawful and unenforceable** [3-5]. The issuer argued that changes in the interpretation of Islamic finance in the UAE meant the sukuk was no longer Shariah-compliant [5]. Specifically, Dana Gas claimed that the sukuk featured a mechanism guaranteeing the face amount of the certificates (the principal) through the issuer's purchase undertaking, which violated the fundamental risk-sharing and profit-and-loss principles of a traditional Mudaraba partnership [3, 4]. 

This unprecedented declaration sparked a fierce legal dispute with its sukuk holders, **most notably including the global investment firm BlackRock** [1, 2]. Dana Gas initially sought to protect itself by obtaining an interim injunction in the Sharjah Federal Court of First Instance in the UAE to prevent investors from declaring a dissolution event or an event of default [3, 4]. 

The conflict severely tested the intersection and enforceability of **English law versus UAE law** [6]. While Dana Gas pursued its Shariah-invalidity claims in the UAE courts, the core transaction documents of the sukuk were governed by English law [3, 4, 6]. The central legal battleground was the **enforceability of the purchase undertaking (Wa'ad)**—the binding promise made by Dana Gas to buy back the assets at a specified price, which effectively guaranteed the return of principal to investors [1, 2, 7]. In November 2017, the English High Court ruled decisively against Dana Gas on all grounds, upholding the validity of the purchase undertakings [1, 3, 4]. The court established that under English law, the commercial terms of the contract were binding and enforceable, and claims of Shariah non-compliance could not be used as a legal defense to avoid debt repayment [6, 8]. 

Following protracted litigation across both jurisdictions, **the dispute was ultimately settled in 2018 via a new structure** [1, 2, 6]. The restructuring was effected by cancelling the existing convertible sukuk and issuing two new, Shariah-compliant instruments with a five-year maturity [6, 9]. Because Dana Gas did not hold significant fixed assets to use a more secure Ijara (leasing) structure, the parties had to work closely with Islamic scholars to create a novel structure that preserved the Mudaraba element while ensuring strict Shariah compliance [9].

The Dana Gas dispute provides profound lessons on the **"form versus substance" debate** and the evolution of credit risk in Islamic finance [1, 6]. It highlighted the inherent fragility of structures that mimic conventional debt (form) but fail to embody true Islamic economic risk-sharing (substance) [1, 7, 10]. Crucially, the case demonstrated that **investors must actively assess Shariah validity as a distinct credit risk factor**, as the issuer's own challenge to the structure's religious compliance created immense market uncertainty [1, 6]. In response to this event, the industry has seen a rise in "substance scrutiny" from scholars and regulators, and market practitioners now increasingly embed enhanced waivers in sukuk documentation to explicitly prevent obligors from challenging enforceability on Shariah grounds [1, 2, 11].
