# Module 5: Islamic Banking, Treasury, and Liquidity


## 5.1 Balance Sheet Structure

**Trading and Profit-and-Loss Sharing (PLS) Business Models**
Islamic financial institutions (IFIs) operate on a constraint-driven design system that prohibits interest (*riba*), excessive uncertainty (*gharar*), and gambling (*maysir*) [1-3]. Because money is viewed merely as a medium of exchange with no intrinsic time value, IFIs cannot profit from lending [4, 5]. Instead, their balance sheets are structured around two primary business models: trade/lease-based financing and Profit-and-Loss Sharing (PLS) partnerships [6-8]. 

The stylized balance sheet of an Islamic bank differs significantly from a conventional bank. Instead of interest-bearing loans, the asset side consists of sales receivables, leased assets, real estate, and equity/PLS financing [9]. 
*   **PLS Models (Equity-Like):** The cornerstone of Islamic finance theory is risk-sharing [10, 11]. The two main PLS contracts are **Mudarabah** (a silent partnership where the bank provides capital and the entrepreneur provides expertise) and **Musharakah** (a joint venture where both parties contribute capital and share management) [12-15]. In a *Mudarabah*, financial losses are borne entirely by the capital provider (*Rab-al-Mal*), while the manager (*Mudarib*) loses only their time and effort [12, 15].
*   **Trading Models (Debt-Like):** In practice, the vast majority of IFI asset portfolios consist of trade- and lease-based instruments that replicate fixed-income profiles [6, 16-19]. These include **Murabaha** (cost-plus sales with deferred payments), **Ijarah** (leasing), **Salam** (forward sales, often for agriculture), and **Istisna** (manufacturing/construction contracts) [7, 20-22]. While these mimic conventional debt economics, they are legally structured as asset sales or leases, requiring the bank to take temporary or ongoing ownership of the underlying assets [16, 23, 24].

**Profit-Sharing Investment Accounts (PSIA)**
On the liabilities side, IFIs replace conventional interest-bearing deposits with Profit-Sharing Investment Accounts (PSIAs) [25, 26]. PSIAs are fundamentally different from conventional deposits because they are designed to absorb losses; they do not guarantee the principal or a fixed return [27, 28].
*   **Structure and Types:** PSIAs are usually structured as *Mudarabah* or *Wakalah* (agency) contracts [29-31]. They are divided into **Unrestricted PSIAs (UPSIA)**, where funds are commingled with the bank's own equity and invested at the bank's discretion, and **Restricted PSIAs (RPSIA)**, where investors specify exact investment parameters and the funds are held off-balance-sheet [32-36].
*   **Displaced Commercial Risk (DCR) and Reserves:** While PSIA holders are theoretically supposed to bear the financial risks of the underlying assets, Islamic banks face massive withdrawal risk (depositor flight) if their returns fall below conventional market benchmarks [17, 37, 38]. To remain competitive, IFIs often smooth payouts by giving up part of their own shareholder profits, a phenomenon known as **Displaced Commercial Risk (DCR)** [31, 39, 40]. To manage this, IFIs establish a **Profit Equalization Reserve (PER)** (appropriated before the bank's profit share is calculated) and an **Investment Risk Reserve (IRR)** (appropriated from the investors' share to cushion against future capital losses) [32, 41-43].
*   **Regulatory Treatment and Defaults:** Because banks often smooth returns, regulators struggle with how to treat PSIAs for capital adequacy. The "Alpha factor" is used to determine what percentage of the risk is actually borne by the PSIA holders versus the bank's shareholders [44-46]. Furthermore, a negative return on a PSIA is not considered a default by rating agencies like Moody's, unless there is proven misconduct, negligence, or breach of contract by the IFI [28].

**Contract-Specific Operational Risks**
Because IFIs must embed their financing within real economic activities, they face unique operational and legal risks not found in conventional banking [25, 47, 48].
*   **Ijarah (Leasing) Risks:** In an *Ijarah* contract, the bank retains legal ownership of the asset and is strictly responsible for its major maintenance, insurance (*Takaful*), and ownership taxes [49-52]. Most critically, if the leased asset (e.g., a factory or aircraft) is totally destroyed, the lease terminates and the rent must cease immediately, placing heavy reliance on robust *Takaful* and "Total Loss" clauses [50, 53-55]. 
*   **Murabaha and Tawarruq Risks:** In a *Murabaha* transaction, the bank must genuinely purchase and take title to the asset before selling it to the client. Failure to properly sequence this—selling the asset before owning it—renders the contract void as *Riba* [56, 57]. For liquidity management, banks heavily use *Tawarruq* (commodity Murabaha), which relies on global commodity platforms (like the London Metal Exchange). This heavy reliance amplifies systemic operational risks if commodity trading platforms experience disruptions [58, 59].
*   **PLS (Mudarabah/Musharakah) Risks:** Equity-like financing suffers from severe asymmetric information, moral hazard, and adverse selection [60-62]. Because the bank bears all financial losses in a *Mudarabah*, entrepreneurs have a strong incentive to underreport profits, inflate costs, or engage in excessive risk-taking, necessitating high monitoring costs for the IFI [62-65].
*   **Shariah Non-Compliance Risk:** An overarching operational risk is the failure to comply with Shariah parameters [66-68]. If a transaction is found to violate Islamic principles (e.g., charging compounding late fees or failing to prove asset ownership), the income is deemed *haram* (impermissible). It cannot be recognized as profit and must be donated to charity, which directly impacts the bank's profitability and damages its market reputation [23, 49, 66].


## 5.2 Core Application Areas

**Islamic finance** is a holistic economic system grounded in Shariah jurisprudence. It inherently views money purely as a medium of exchange rather than a commodity with intrinsic value. Consequently, the system strictly prohibits the charging of interest (**Riba**), excessive uncertainty (**Gharar**), and speculation or gambling (**Maysir**) [1-5]. By demanding that all financial transactions be tied to tangible assets, services, or risk-sharing partnerships, Islamic finance ensures that wealth generation serves the real economy [6-10]. 

Below is an expansion on the core applications of Islamic finance across various domains:

### Retail and Corporate Banking
Islamic banking substitutes conventional debtor-creditor loans with active trade, leasing, and partnership contracts [11-13]. 
*   **Murabaha (Cost-Plus Sale):** This is the predominant structure for retail mortgages, auto finance, and corporate asset acquisition. The bank purchases an asset requested by the customer and assumes legal ownership and risk, however briefly, before reselling it to the customer at a pre-agreed markup payable in installments [14-22]. 
*   **Tawarruq (Commodity Murabaha):** Widely used for liquidity management, credit cards, and corporate working capital. To generate cash, the bank sells a highly liquid commodity (e.g., metals) to the client on a deferred-payment basis; the client then immediately sells it to a third party for spot cash [23-29]. *Note: Organized Tawarruq faces criticism from Shariah scholars who argue it functions as a synthetic cash loan (a legal trick, or hilah) lacking genuine economic substance* [30-37].
*   **Ijarah (Leasing):** Used heavily for corporate infrastructure, aircraft, and real estate. The bank maintains ownership of the asset and leases its usufruct (use) to the client for a rental fee, assuming maintenance and ownership risks. It often concludes with a transfer of title (Ijarah wa Iqtina) [13, 33, 38-41].
*   **Deposits:** Current accounts operate as **Qard Hasan** (benevolent, interest-free loans), ensuring capital preservation, while savings accounts rely on **Mudarabah** (profit-sharing) or **Wakala** (agency), exposing depositors to the bank's investment risks and returns [15, 42-46].

### Trade Finance
Trade finance is a natural application for Islamic finance because it inherently involves physical goods and tangible economic transactions [7, 47, 48].
*   **Salam (Forward Financing):** Crucial for agricultural and pre-export finance, *Salam* allows the financier to pay spot cash in advance for specified commodities to be delivered at a future date. This provides vital working capital to producers before production is complete [41, 49-54]. 
*   **Institutional Facilitation:** Entities like the International Islamic Trade Finance Corporation (ITFC) heavily deploy *Murabaha* and *Wakala* structures to provide import/export financing, LC confirmations, and SME capacity building across member countries [48, 55-59].
*   **Digital Innovation:** The sector is shifting toward embedded finance, utilizing blockchain and smart contracts to immutably track physical goods. This solves execution complexities and guarantees that the asset-backing requirements of trade contracts are verified in real-time [60-62].

### Project Finance and Infrastructure (PPPs)
Infrastructure Public-Private Partnerships (PPPs) are a perfect fit for Islamic finance because their core characteristics—"ring-fenced" assets, tangible economic development, and distributed risk—align seamlessly with Shariah [63-68]. 
*   **Istisna' (Construction Financing):** Because assets like hospitals or power plants do not exist at the time of financing, Islamic financiers use *Istisna'*—a contract to procure and construct an asset to exact specifications for a fixed deferred price. The financier acts as the purchaser and usually appoints the project's Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) as its agent to oversee the Engineering, Procurement, and Construction (EPC) contractor [53, 69-73]. 
*   **Hybrid Structures:** Practitioners frequently blend contracts, utilizing *Istisna'* during the greenfield construction phase, which transitions into an *Ijarah* (lease) once the project becomes operational and generates cash flow (e.g., the Madinah Airport expansion and Konya Hospital in Turkey) [68, 74-79].
*   **Musharakah (Joint Venture):** In projects like the Karachi Thatta Dual Carriageway, the state and the private sector pool resources (e.g., the state provides land, the financier provides capital). They share profits based on equity and manage the asset together [80-83].
*   **Parallel Financing:** It is highly common for Islamic and conventional lenders to co-finance large PPPs. Shariah does not prohibit Islamic financiers from participating in a project alongside interest-based lenders, provided the Islamic tranche is ring-fenced to a permissible asset and structured via inter-creditor agreements that guarantee *pari passu* (equal footing) commercial rights [68, 84-88].

### Asset Management and Funds (IFSB-6)
Governed by frameworks such as the **Islamic Financial Services Board's IFSB-6** standard, Islamic Collective Investment Schemes (ICIS) pool capital to acquire proportionate ownership in undivided, Shariah-compliant assets [89-94].
*   **Ethical Screening:** Portfolios undergo rigorous Shariah screening to eliminate prohibited (*Haram*) activities (e.g., alcohol, gambling, weapons, conventional banking). Furthermore, strict financial ratios are enforced: a target company's interest-bearing debt and liquid assets generally cannot exceed 30–33% of its market capitalization [95-103]. 
*   **Purification:** Any incidental, impure income generated by a portfolio company must be quantified and "purified" by the fund through mandatory charitable donations [104-106].
*   **GP/LP Structuring:** Private equity and venture capital funds utilize **Mudarabah** or **Wakala**. In a *Wakala bi al-Istithmar* (investment agency) structure, the General Partner (Wakeel) manages the fund for a fixed management fee. If the fund exceeds a targeted profit hurdle, the Wakeel retains the excess as an incentive or carried interest, aligning with conventional waterfall mechanics without violating Shariah [107-113].
*   **Governance:** A crucial governance focus is protecting Investment Account Holders (IAHs) from displaced commercial risk. IFSB guidelines require transparent management of the Profit Equalization Reserve (PER) and Investment Risk Reserve (IRR), which are used to smooth dividend payouts and cushion against capital losses [114-119].

### Takaful (Islamic Insurance)
Conventional insurance involves the buying and selling of risk, which Shariah categorizes as impermissible due to the presence of *Gharar* (uncertainty), *Maysir* (gambling), and *Riba* (investing premiums in interest-bearing bonds) [120-125]. 
*   **Tabarru' (Donation Pool):** Takaful mitigates these prohibitions by operating on a mutual, cooperative framework. Participants voluntarily donate their premiums as **Tabarru'** into a common risk pool. Because the funds are given as charitable donations for mutual assistance rather than a commercial exchange, the elements of *Gharar* and *Maysir* are nullified [119, 126-132]. 
*   **The Wakeel (Operator):** To manage the underwriting and investments, a commercial Takaful operator is appointed. Under the **Wakala model**, the operator (Wakeel) charges a transparent, upfront agency fee to manage the pool but has no claim on the underwriting surplus. Alternatively, under the **Mudarabah model**, the operator manages the investments and takes a share of the investment profits [133-139].
*   **Qard Hasan and Surplus:** The risk pool belongs entirely to the participants. If claims exceed the pool's reserves, creating a deficit, the Takaful operator's shareholders are legally obligated to inject a **Qard Hasan** (an interest-free benevolent loan) to keep the pool solvent. This loan is later recovered from future underwriting surpluses. Any remaining surplus after obligations are met is redistributed back to the participants, reinforcing the mutual nature of the system [140-145].


## 5.3 Treasury and Liquidity Management

Treasury and liquidity management represents a fundamental differentiator between Islamic and conventional finance. Because Islamic finance operates under strict Shariah principles—most notably the prohibition of *riba* (interest)—financial institutions cannot simply rent out or borrow money [1-3]. This constraint fundamentally alters how Islamic banks manage their balance sheets, leading to unique structural challenges and the development of specialized, Shariah-compliant liquidity instruments [4-6]. 

**Inability to Use Conventional Money-Market Instruments**
Conventional banks rely heavily on interest-bearing treasury bills, government bonds, interbank lending, and conventional repurchase agreements (repos) to manage short-term funding [6, 7]. Islamic financial institutions (IFIs) are prohibited from accessing these interest-based instruments [6, 8]. Furthermore, many jurisdictions lack a Shariah-compliant Lender of Last Resort (LOLR) facility, meaning central banks often cannot provide emergency liquidity to IFIs using traditional discount windows [7, 9]. 

**Structural Inefficiencies**
The prohibition on conventional money-market tools creates several structural inefficiencies for Islamic banks:
*   **High Excess Reserves:** Due to a scarcity of Shariah-compliant High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA) and short-term investment options, IFIs are forced to hold disproportionately high levels of idle cash and central bank reserves [8, 10-12]. This excess liquidity buffer ensures near-term resilience but acts as a drag on overall capital efficiency and profitability [8, 10, 13].
*   **Maturity Mismatches:** IFIs typically fund long-term, illiquid assets (like real estate and project finance) using short-term, demand-based deposits (such as profit-sharing investment accounts or current accounts), exposing them to significant withdrawal and liquidity risks [14-17].
*   **Illiquid Secondary Markets:** The secondary market for Islamic instruments is shallow and fragmented [18, 19]. Because many sukuk (Islamic certificates) are debt-based and subject to strict Shariah trading constraints (which prohibit trading debt except at par value), they are generally non-tradable and held to maturity by investors [10, 20-23].
*   **Jurisdictional Fragmentation:** Differing interpretations of Shariah compliance across regions—such as Malaysia being more permissive of certain hybrid debt structures than the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC)—hinder the harmonization of cross-border liquidity pools [19, 24-26]. 

To overcome these inefficiencies, the Islamic finance industry has engineered several specialized treasury and liquidity instruments:

**Commodity Murabaha Placements (Tawarruq)**
**Commodity Murabaha** (often referred to as organized *Tawarruq*) is the dominant instrument used by Islamic treasuries to generate short-term cash liquidity [27-30]. 
*   **How it works:** The bank purchases a highly liquid commodity (such as metals from the London Metal Exchange) on a spot basis, then sells it to the client or counterparty at a markup on a deferred payment basis. The client immediately sells the commodity to a third party for spot cash [27, 31-38]. 
*   **Drawbacks:** While providing crucial working capital, it involves transaction costs (brokerage fees) and counterparty risks [39, 40]. Furthermore, it is heavily criticized by Shariah purists as a "legal trick" (*hilah*) that mimics an interest-bearing loan without introducing genuine economic risk-sharing [27, 28, 41-43].

**Short-Term Wakala Deposits**
**Wakala** (agency) contracts have gained significant momentum as an alternative to Commodity Murabaha for interbank placements and deposit structuring [29, 44-46]. 
*   **How it works:** An investor (principal) appoints the bank (agent) to manage surplus funds by investing them in a Shariah-compliant portfolio for a specific fee [44, 47-49]. The principal receives the expected profit, and the bank retains any excess as an incentive fee [50, 51]. 
*   **Applications:** It outpaces Commodity Murabaha in execution time by avoiding complex commodity trades [52]. Central banks, such as the Central Bank of Bahrain and the Bank of England, have established Wakalah-based liquidity facilities to help IFIs securely park overnight or short-term excess cash [53-56].

**IILM Sukuk**
To address the severe shortage of cross-border, short-term HQLA, central banks and multilateral organizations established the **International Islamic Liquidity Management Corporation (IILM)** [18, 57, 58]. 
*   **Role:** The IILM issues highly rated (A-1/F1), short-term *sukuk* with maturities ranging from two weeks to one year [58-60]. 
*   **Impact:** Backed by sovereign and supranational assets, IILM sukuk provide a globally accepted, tradable benchmark for Islamic liquidity, allowing banks to manage short-term funding without resorting to conventional interbank markets [20, 57, 61-63]. 

**Malaysian Repo Tools**
Malaysia operates one of the deepest Islamic money markets globally and has pioneered alternatives to conventional repos [64]. 
*   **Instruments:** Malaysia utilizes Sell and Buy Back Agreements (SBBA) and has introduced the Islamic Collateralised Funding Policy Document (ICF PD) [65-67]. 
*   **Impact:** These tools incorporate advanced repo features like margining and asset substitution, which successfully surged Malaysia's secured interbank transaction volumes from RM12.6 billion in 2023 to RM42.6 billion in 2024 [65-67]. However, tools like the SBBA face cross-border limitations, as they are generally not viewed as Shariah-compliant by GCC scholars [24].

**Collateralised Murabaha**
To solve the lack of standardization in Islamic repos, particularly in the Middle East, the **Collateralised Murabaha** was developed [68, 69]. This mechanism combines the deferred-payment structure of a Murabaha transaction with the pledging of collateral, providing secured funding and allowing banks to manage counterparty risk securely in the interbank market [29, 68-70].

**IIFM Master Agreements**
Because structural complexity and bespoke contracts impede secondary market liquidity, the **International Islamic Financial Market (IIFM)** acts as a global standard-setting body to unify documentation [71, 72]. 
*   **Standardization:** The IIFM has developed essential templates like the **Master Agreement for Treasury Placement (MATP)**, the Master Collateralized Murabahah Agreement (MCMA), and the ISDA/IIFM Tahawwut Master Agreement for Islamic hedging/derivatives [45, 68, 73-76]. 
*   **Benefit:** These master agreements reduce legal and Shariah-challenge risks, lower transaction costs, and establish the standardized framework necessary for a functioning global Islamic treasury market [29, 77].


## 5.4 Family Office Relevance

For a family office, Islamic structuring goes far beyond simply avoiding prohibited income. It operates as a strict, constraint-driven design system that fundamentally alters the mechanics of capital allocation, risk transfer, and enforceability. These ethical and jurisprudential constraints directly impact **liquidity tools, leverage limits, deal documentation, governance, private market pacing, exit timing, and portfolio financing** [1, 2]. 

**Liquidity Tools**
Family offices cannot utilize conventional interest-bearing deposits, treasury bills, or repos for short-term cash management, as these violate the prohibition of *riba* (interest/usury) [3-5]. Instead, they must rely on Shariah-compliant alternatives. The most common tools are **Commodity Murabaha (Tawarruq)**, **short-term Wakala (agency) deposits**, and **short-term Sukuk** (such as those issued by the International Islamic Liquidity Management Corporation) [6-8]. However, family offices must be cautious: Tawarruq is a controversial structure that closely replicates conventional cash loans through a sequence of commodity sales. It is heavily criticized by Shariah purists and requires stringent governance to ensure it is legally defensible and relies on genuine third-party trading [9-11]. Overall, the structural shortage of high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) in Islamic markets means family offices may struggle to rapidly convert holdings to cash without incurring opportunity costs or taking on governance risks [12, 13].

**Leverage Limits**
Islamic finance strictly prohibits interest-based borrowing, profoundly impacting the highly leveraged models typical of conventional private equity and real estate [14, 15]. 
*   **Fund-Level Leverage:** Subscription lines cannot be interest-based; they must be structured via Shariah-compliant facilities such as *Murabaha* or *Musharaka* [14, 15]. 
*   **Portfolio-Level Leverage:** To ensure compliance, target companies are subjected to strict financial screening. Standards set by bodies like AAOIFI, MSCI, and DJIM mandate that **interest-bearing debt must not exceed 33% (or 33.33%) of the company’s total assets or trailing market capitalization** [16-19]. This forces family offices to abandon highly leveraged buyouts in favor of "growth capital" or all-equity deals, relying on genuine value creation rather than debt engineering to generate returns [14, 20, 21].

**Deal Documentation**
Islamic deal documentation is fundamentally different because it replaces standard loan agreements with a matrix of trade, lease, and partnership contracts (e.g., *Murabaha*, *Ijara*, *Mudaraba*, and *Musharaka*) [22, 23]. Because Islamic finance requires **asset linkage** and **material finality**, contracts must evidence the actual transfer of ownership, constructive possession, and the assumption of commercial risk [24, 25]. Conventional protections that eliminate risk—such as guaranteed minimum returns, fixed penalty clauses for late payments (unless paid to charity), and preferred shares with fixed dividends—are strictly prohibited as they violate profit-and-loss sharing (*PLS*) principles [26-28]. To achieve commercial predictability without violating Shariah, documentation relies heavily on mechanisms like *Wa'ad* (unilateral promises) and purchase undertakings, though these must be drafted meticulously to remain legally enforceable [22, 29, 30].

**Governance**
Family offices must implement a robust Shariah governance framework, typically guided by an independent **Shariah Supervisory Board (SSB)** [31, 32]. The SSB holds the authority to issue binding *Fatwas* (legal opinions) approving or rejecting transactions [32, 33]. This introduces a layer of ongoing operational oversight, requiring mandatory **Shariah audits** to ensure that investments do not drift into non-compliance [31, 34, 35]. If a portfolio company breaches financial ratios (e.g., taking on debt above 33%) or engages in prohibited activities, the SSB can force a mandatory divestment—sometimes within 90 days—adding significant governance risk to the portfolio [36-38]. Furthermore, because Islamic law is open to interpretation, family offices face "scholar concentration risk" and jurisdictional divergence, where a structure approved in Malaysia might be rejected in the GCC [39, 40].

**Private Market Pacing**
The requirement for "patient capital" naturally aligns with Islamic risk-sharing, but Shariah constraints can considerably slow down private market pacing [41]. The investable universe is inherently smaller due to the exclusion of "haram" sectors (alcohol, gambling, conventional finance, etc.) and the strict debt-screening criteria, necessitating longer due diligence phases [42-44]. Furthermore, bridge financing for capital calls cannot rely on rapid, conventional interest-bearing loans. It requires alternative structures like *Qard* (interest-free loans) or *Mudaraba*, which can complicate funding timelines and hinder the rapid scaling expected in traditional venture capital [41, 45, 46].

**Exit Timing**
Shariah rules directly influence when and how a family office can exit an investment. **Structuring a guaranteed exit price upfront is prohibited**, as it eliminates the risk of sharing a loss [27]. A common Shariah-compliant exit strategy is **Diminishing Musharaka**, where the entrepreneur gradually buys back the investor's equity at fair market value [10, 47, 48]. Unlike conventional funds that can rapidly divest non-performing assets or trade distressed debt at a discount, Islamic investors face strict prohibitions on the sale of debt (*Bay' al-Dayn*) [49-51]. Consequently, family offices may be locked into holding shares for a fixed period or face a restricted pool of buyers, since the exit route (like a trade sale or IPO) must itself pass Shariah scrutiny to ensure the buyer's funds and the post-exit structure are compliant [51-53].

**Portfolio Financing and Cleansing**
For ongoing portfolio management, any financing required by target companies must utilize Islamic debt replacements (like *Murabaha* or *Ijara*) which involve tangible assets, preventing the use of standard corporate overdrafts [8, 54, 55]. Most importantly, family offices are subject to rigorous **income purification** rules. While incidental income from impermissible sources (such as interest on cash balances or incidental non-halal business) is tolerated up to a strict 5% threshold, any such tainted income must be meticulously calculated, isolated, and donated to charity [56-60]. **This mandatory purification process reduces the net yield** to the family office and requires continuous, active monitoring of the portfolio's revenue streams [59, 61].
