# Module 6: Sukuk and Public Capital Markets


## 6.1 Sukuk Definition and Economic Role

**Sukuk**, often referred to as "Shariah-compliant bonds," are formally defined as Islamic financial certificates that represent an undivided ownership share in an underlying tangible asset, project, or specialized investing activity [1], [2], [3]. The global standard for these instruments is established by the **Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI) under Shari'ah Standard No. 17 (Investment Sukuk)**. AAOIFI SS 17 strictly defines sukuk as "certificates of equal value representing undivided shares in ownership of tangible assets, usufruct and services or (in the ownership of) the assets of particular projects or special investment activity" [4], [3], [5], [6], [7].

**The Distinction from Conventional Bonds**
While sukuk serve a similar economic function to conventional bonds in raising capital, their legal and structural foundations are fundamentally different. A conventional bond is a **pure debt obligation (an IOU) in which the issuer borrows money and promises to repay the principal with interest** [8], [9], [10], [7], [11]. 

Because Islamic law explicitly forbids the charging or paying of interest (*riba*), **sukuk must be backed by real, Shariah-compliant assets or business ventures** [12], [1], [2], [13], [14]. Instead of receiving fixed interest payments, sukuk investors earn returns generated by the actual performance of the underlying asset—such as rental income, profit-sharing, or a combination thereof [1], [15], [16], [17], [10]. Consequently, sukuk holders inherently share in the commercial risks and rewards associated with the asset's ownership, aligning with the Islamic principle of risk-sharing [1], [18], [19], [20].

**Market Scale and Growth**
The global sukuk market has achieved massive scale, transitioning from a niche ethical product to a mainstream fixture in global capital markets. According to the International Islamic Financial Market (IIFM), **total global sukuk issuances reached US$ 205 billion in 2024** [21], [22], [23], [24], [25]. 

Within this total, **international sukuk issuances hit a record high of US$ 65.6 billion** in 2024, representing a 24.5% increase from the previous year, driven by favorable global liquidity conditions and declining funding costs [21], [26], [23], [24]. As a result of this continued momentum, **total outstanding sukuk surpassed the US$ 900 billion mark** [27], [22], [26], [28], with several broader industry reports projecting or confirming the market successfully crossing the US$ 1 trillion threshold globally in 2024 [29], [30], [31], [32], [33], [34], [35].

**Sovereign Sukuk as the Market Anchor**
**Sovereign sukuk act as the critical anchor segment** for the broader Islamic capital markets [22], [36]. For governments, issuing sovereign sukuk is a strategic tool to diversify their investor base, allowing them to tap into vast pools of Islamic liquidity and ethical investors who avoid conventional interest-bearing debt [37], [38], [39]. 

More importantly, sovereign issuances lay the groundwork for a healthy financial ecosystem. They establish **vital pricing benchmarks and local-currency yield curves** that facilitate and encourage private sector and corporate issuances [40], [22], [41], [42], [43]. Additionally, sovereign sukuk provide essential high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) that central banks and Islamic financial institutions use for robust liquidity management and monetary policy operations [44], [45], [43], [46].


## 6.2 Asset-Based vs Asset-Backed: The Critical Distinction

The most important structural distinction in the Islamic capital markets revolves around how a *sukuk* (Islamic investment certificate) allocates risk and ownership between the issuer and the investors. While both structures aim to comply with Islamic prohibitions on interest (*riba*) and excessive uncertainty (*gharar*), they function very differently in practice. 

**Asset-Based Sukuk (Dominant, Credit Reliance)**
The vast majority of sukuk issued today—up to 90% or 95% of the market—are structured as asset-based securities [1, 2]. In an asset-based structure, the originator identifies a pool of Shariah-compliant assets to facilitate the transaction, but **no "true sale" or legal transfer of ownership takes place** [3-5]. Instead, investors are only granted a "beneficial ownership" interest in the assets [3, 4]. 

Because legal title remains with the originator on their balance sheet [3], investors do not bear the genuine risks of owning the asset [6]. More importantly, **investors have no direct recourse to the underlying assets in the event of a default** [3, 7, 8]. Instead, the investors rely entirely on the creditworthiness of the originator (the obligor) [9-11]. To mimic the fixed-income characteristics and capital preservation of a conventional bond, these structures utilize a **"purchase undertaking"** (a binding promise) where the originator agrees to buy back the assets at maturity or upon default for a predetermined fixed price—usually the exact face value of the original investment [2, 4, 12-15]. In substance, this replicates a conventional unsecured corporate loan [16-19].

**Asset-Backed Sukuk (True Sale, Genuine Risk Transfer)**
Conversely, an asset-backed sukuk involves a **"true sale"** where the legal title and ownership of the underlying assets are genuinely transferred from the originator to a bankruptcy-remote Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) [7, 8, 10, 20]. 

In this structure, the core Islamic principle of *al-ghunm wa al-ghurm* (entitlement to gain is justified only by bearing risk) is fully realized [21]. Sukuk holders assume the genuine risks and rewards associated with the asset's performance [7, 20]. **There is no guarantee of principal return**; if the underlying asset underperforms, is damaged, or drops in market value, the investors bear the financial loss [22-25]. Because the assets are completely separated from the originator's books, investors have direct recourse to the assets themselves, shielding them from the originator's potential bankruptcy [7, 8, 20, 23]. While highly favored by Shariah purists for their authenticity, asset-backed sukuk are relatively rare due to legal complexities and lower investor appetite for actual equity-like risk [8, 10, 22, 26].

**The 2008 AAOIFI Sukuk Statement**
The tension between these two structures reached a boiling point in late 2007 when Sheikh Muhammad Taqi Usmani, Chairman of the Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI) Shariah board, publicly estimated that 85% of outstanding sukuk failed to comply with core Shariah principles [9, 27-30]. He criticized asset-based structures—particularly those using *mudaraba* and *musharaka* (partnership) contracts—for utilizing purchase undertakings that guaranteed the return of principal, arguing they were effectively conventional debt instruments [9, 14, 31].

In response, AAOIFI issued a landmark pronouncement in February 2008 outlining strict rules to realign the market [30, 32]. The 2008 Statement mandated that:
*   Sukuk must represent genuine ownership with all its rights and obligations [33, 34].
*   Managers or agents cannot offer loans to investors to cover earnings shortfalls [35].
*   **Most crucially, originators cannot undertake to repurchase equity-based assets at their nominal (face) value at maturity or default** [14, 36]. Any repurchase must be executed at the asset's net, market, or fair value at the time of the sale, forcing investors to bear actual market risk [14, 37].

The 2008 Statement caused a sharp decline in *musharaka* and *mudaraba* sukuk issuances [38-40], leading the market to heavily favor *ijara* (lease-based) structures where it was easier to compliantly structure predictable, bond-like returns [31, 41].

**The AAOIFI Standard 62 Debate**
The debate between legal form and economic substance is currently being tested again with AAOIFI's proposed Shariah Standard No. 62, drafted in late 2023 [42, 43]. Standard 62 seeks to completely overhaul the market by **mandating the actual legal transfer of ownership (true sale) of underlying assets from issuers to investors**, eliminating implicit and explicit capital guarantees, and effectively forcing the market into true asset-backed structures [42, 43].

This has sparked a massive debate regarding **Shariah Authenticity vs. Market Stability** [44, 45]:
*   **Shariah Authenticity:** Standard 62 successfully realigns sukuk with their intended religious purpose as risk-sharing investment certificates [42, 46]. It answers long-standing criticisms that Islamic finance is merely conventional finance repackaged with Arabic terminology [46, 47].
*   **Market Stability & Disruption:** Industry participants heavily warn that a strict implementation of Standard 62 could destabilize the $1.2 trillion sukuk market [46, 48, 49]. True asset sales incur massive transaction costs, trigger heavy tax implications, and are legally difficult in jurisdictions that restrict foreign ownership of sovereign assets [50]. 
*   **Credit Ratings and Fragmentation:** If sukuk transition to variable-return, genuine risk-sharing instruments, traditional credit rating agencies may view them as equity rather than debt, rendering them "unrateable" under standard fixed-income methodologies [48, 51]. Furthermore, analysts fear severe market fragmentation; GCC countries may strictly adopt Standard 62, while major hubs like Malaysia—which relies on its own flexible Shariah framework—may ignore it, fracturing global Islamic liquidity [46, 52, 53]. 

Consequently, policymakers and regulators are currently exploring "phased adoption" frameworks to balance the religious mandate for asset-backed authenticity against the commercial need for market continuity and stable pricing [48, 54].


## 6.3 Sukuk Structure Menu

**Ijara Sukuk (Lease-Based)**
The Ijara Sukuk functions as a lease-based financial mechanism where an originator sells a tangible asset to a Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV), which subsequently leases it back to the originator [1-3]. The periodic rental payments form the returns passed on to the Sukuk holders [2, 3]. Because this structure represents undivided beneficial ownership in the usufruct of physical assets rather than just financial debt, it is **highly tradable in secondary markets** [4-7]. For this reason, along with its easily understood lease economics, Ijara is the **most widely used structure for sovereign issuances**, prominently utilized by governments such as the UK, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE [5, 6, 8-10].

**Wakala Sukuk (Agency-Based)**
Wakala Sukuk operate on an investment agency contract where investors appoint an agent (*wakeel*) to manage funds with the goal of generating an expected profit return [11-14]. This structure provides **high flexibility for diversifying pools of assets**, allowing originators (such as Islamic banks) to bundle various Shariah-compliant investments—including lease contracts, equities, and Murabaha receivables—when a single large tangible asset is unavailable [4, 6, 12, 15-17]. Because the agent exercises significant control over the portfolio, this structure requires **robust and unambiguous agency agreements** that clearly define the investment criteria, duration, fee structure, and termination conditions [6, 18, 19]. In this arrangement, the investors receive the pre-agreed profit return, while the *wakeel* typically retains any excess profit as a performance incentive fee [15, 20, 21].

**Murabaha & Salam Sukuk (Sale-Based & Debt)**
Murabaha Sukuk are based on cost-plus-profit sales, where the issuer acquires commodities and sells them at a disclosed markup on deferred payment terms [22-24]. Salam Sukuk involve a forward sale where payment is made upfront for future delivery of goods [25-27]. Both structures face **severe tradability constraints because their issuance predominantly generates debt receivables** rather than tangible ownership [4, 6, 27-30]. Under Islamic jurisprudence, trading in debt at a discount or premium is considered *riba* (interest). **AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 59 on the Sale of Debt (Bay' al-Dayn)** explicitly restricts the secondary market trading of such monetary debts, stipulating they cannot be traded except strictly at par value [31-34]. Therefore, these Sukuk can generally only become negotiable if they constitute a highly restricted, small fraction of a larger portfolio that is predominantly backed by tangible assets [29, 35-38].

**Musharaka & Mudaraba Sukuk (Equity-Based Partnerships)**
These are **equity-based partnership structures** built on the core Islamic principle of profit and loss sharing (PLS) [39-44]. Musharaka is a joint venture where all parties contribute capital and share profits and losses, while Mudaraba is a trustee partnership involving a capital provider (*Rab-al-Maal*) and an entrepreneur/manager (*Mudarib*) [45-50]. In 2008, the AAOIFI issued a critical statement asserting that up to 85% of existing Sukuk failed **Shariah substance requirements** because they utilized fixed-price purchase undertakings [51-55]. These undertakings virtually guaranteed the return of principal by forcing the originator to buy back the partnership assets at face value, making them economically identical to conventional guaranteed debt [56-61]. To ensure **genuine profit and loss sharing**, the 2008 AAOIFI requirements dictate that any repurchase of the underlying partnership assets must occur at net asset value or fair market value at the time of sale, forcing investors to bear true equity risk [54, 62-65].

**Hybrid Sukuk**
To balance stringent Shariah structural requirements with practical market demands for liquidity, issuers frequently utilize Hybrid Sukuk, which **combine multiple Islamic contracts** (e.g., combining Istisna with Ijara, or Wakala with Murabaha) [4, 31, 59, 66-68]. This approach allows originators to tap into capital markets even if their balance sheets are heavy with non-tradable debt receivables. However, to ensure these hybrid instruments remain tradable in the secondary market, Shariah scholars mandate a **minimum tangible asset ratio**—commonly ranging from **33% to 51%** of the total underlying portfolio—to ensure the Sukuk represents a genuine ownership interest rather than pure debt [31, 69-74].


## 6.4 Secondary Market Issues

**AAOIFI Shariah Standard 59 and the Sale of Debt (*Bay' al-Dayn*)**
In Islamic finance, the secondary market is heavily governed by the prohibition of *riba* (interest) and the requirement that transactions be linked to real economic activity [1]. **AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 59 on the Sale of Debt** establishes strict boundaries for trading financial obligations. The standard explicitly prohibits selling a monetary debt for cash or another monetary debt, as exchanging money for money at a discount or premium constitutes *riba* [2]. While a monetary debt can be sold for a commodity, usufruct, or service with spot delivery, it cannot be sold for a commodity with deferred delivery, nor can it be sold to a third party if it involves excessive *gharar* (uncertainty) [3, 4]. 

**Tradability Requirements for Tangible Assets and Usufruct Interests**
Because trading pure debt is prohibited, for an Islamic financial instrument (like a Sukuk) to be freely negotiable in the secondary market, it must represent an undivided ownership interest in tangible assets, usufructs (the right to use an asset), or services [5-8]. 

If a portfolio is mixed—containing cash, debt receivables, and physical assets—it can only be traded if it meets specific **tangibility ratios**. SS 59 mandates that tangible assets and usufructs must form a substantial portion of the portfolio. While historically some scholars accepted a minimum of 30% tangible assets, others required 51% to 70% [7, 9-11]. Under SS 59, the ratio of tangible assets must generally exceed 50% for an entity's assets to be freely securitized and traded [12]. However, the standard stipulates an absolute floor: **the ratio of tangible assets must never fall below 33% of the total asset value at any time** [13].

**How This Shapes Portfolio Composition**
The strict tradability requirements of SS 59 profoundly shape how issuers construct and manage their asset portfolios:
*   **Hybrid Structuring:** To achieve desired cash-flow targets while remaining tradable, issuers frequently design "hybrid sukuk." These portfolios combine non-tradable debt contracts (like *Murabaha* or *Istisna'a*) with tradable, asset-backed contracts (like *Ijara* or equities) to ensure the tangible portion meets the 30–51% threshold [10, 14, 15].
*   **Active Maintenance of Tangibility:** SS 59 introduced a dynamic requirement meaning the tangibility ratio is not just an issuance threshold, but an ongoing maintenance requirement [15]. If the tangible asset value drops below 33% during the life of the instrument, the Sukuk becomes non-negotiable (except under the strict rules of debt trading at par value) [13]. If listed on an exchange, a Sukuk breaching this threshold must be delisted, forcing issuers to actively manage asset compositions and substitute assets to maintain liquidity [13, 16].
*   **Use of Usufructs (*Manafa'a*):** When physical assets are unavailable or encumbered, portfolio managers can utilize intangible usufruct rights (such as airtime vouchers, toll road concessions, or electricity tariffs) to satisfy the tangible asset requirement, allowing the portfolio to be traded [17-19].

**Limitations on Certain Structures**
The prohibition on the sale of debt and the tangibility requirements severely limit the liquidity and secondary market viability of several Islamic financial structures:
*   **Murabaha Sukuk:** *Murabaha* is a cost-plus deferred payment sale, meaning it inherently creates a monetary debt receivable [20, 21]. Because they represent debt rather than physical assets, *Murabaha* Sukuk cannot be traded on the secondary market at a premium or discount [22-24]. They must be held to maturity or traded only at par value, making them highly illiquid [21, 24]. (An exception exists in Malaysia, where local jurists have historically permitted the trading of debt (*bay' al-dayn*), allowing secondary trading of *Murabaha* Sukuk, though this is rejected by GCC scholars [21, 25]).
*   **Salam and Istisna'a Sukuk:** *Salam* contracts involve advance payment for the future delivery of goods. Because they represent a debt liability for future delivery, *Salam* certificates are strictly non-tradable [26-28]. Similarly, *Istisna'a* (manufacturing) certificates can only be traded once the funds have been converted into owned physical assets; if the asset has already been delivered to the ultimate buyer, the certificate represents a monetary debt and loses its tradability [28, 29].
*   **Distressed Debt and Private Credit:** The rules under SS 59 deeply constrain distressed debt strategies. In conventional finance, distressed receivables are frequently bought and sold at a discount. In Islamic private markets, the discounted purchase of receivables is forbidden, forcing restructuring strategies to rely on complex asset sales, novation, or equity-based restructurings rather than simple debt trading [30, 31].


## 6.5 Equity Screening and Listed Products

The global Islamic equity market is a massive and rapidly maturing segment of the financial system, acting as the gatekeeper for an estimated **USD 4.3 trillion screened universe** [1, 2]. To ensure that investments align with Islamic law, stock selection relies on a rigorous two-stage architecture: **business activity screening (qualitative)** and **financial ratio screening (quantitative)** [3, 4].

**Business Activity Screens**
The primary step in Shariah screening involves excluding companies whose core business operations fall into prohibited (*haram*) sectors. Common exclusions across all major indices include conventional interest-based financial services (such as banking and insurance), alcohol, pork-related products, gambling and casinos, adult entertainment, weapons and defense, and tobacco [5-9]. 

Because complete purity is rare in modern multinational conglomerates, most Shariah boards and index providers tolerate a *de minimis* level of non-permissible activity. The standard rule is that **revenue from non-permissible sources must not exceed 5% of a company’s total income** [5, 7, 10, 11]. Even when a company passes this screen, Muslim investors are obligated to undergo a **purification process**—calculating the exact percentage of the company's dividend or profit derived from these *haram* sources and donating that equivalent amount to charity [7, 12-15]. 

**Financial Ratio Screens**
Since it is virtually impossible to find public companies completely free of conventional bank accounts or interest-bearing debt, Shariah scholars established quantitative tolerance thresholds [16, 17]. These screens are designed to limit a portfolio's indirect exposure to *riba* (interest) and typically evaluate three core areas:
1. **Leverage (Debt Ratio):** Interest-bearing debt compared to a specific denominator.
2. **Liquidity (Cash Ratio):** Cash and interest-bearing securities compared to a denominator.
3. **Receivables Ratio:** Accounts receivable compared to a denominator [11, 18-21].

Global index providers apply these principles differently, specifically regarding whether they use **Total Assets** or **Market Capitalization** as the denominator for these ratios, which can lead to the same company passing one index's screen while failing another's [10, 11, 22, 23].

**MSCI Islamic Index**
The standard MSCI Islamic Index series employs a balance-sheet approach, using **Total Assets (TA)** as its primary denominator [11, 24]. 
*   **Thresholds:** It restricts total debt, cash and interest-bearing securities, and the sum of accounts receivable plus cash to each be **strictly less than 33.33% of total assets** [11, 25, 26]. 
*   MSCI also applies a stricter business activity screen by specifically excluding companies involved in hotels, cinemas, and music from its investment universe [26]. 
*   To accommodate different investor preferences, MSCI also offers an "M-Series" variant, which calculates ratios against Average Market Capitalization instead of Total Assets [18, 27].

**Dow Jones Islamic Market (DJIM)**
Historically, DJIM structured its financial screens against a **24-month trailing average market capitalization** to smooth out the volatility of daily stock prices [10, 28]. 
*   **Thresholds:** Its traditional rulebook capped total debt, cash plus interest-bearing securities, and accounts receivable at a strict **33%** each [10, 11, 28, 29]. 
*   *Recent Changes:* In a highly notable methodology update in **September 2023**, DJIM **eliminated its cash and accounts receivable screens entirely** [30, 31]. It now evaluates compliance based solely on the 33% debt-to-market-cap limit and the 5% impure income threshold, significantly expanding its pool of eligible equities [30].

**S&P Shariah**
S&P applies a methodology similar to DJIM but traditionally utilizes a **36-month average market capitalization** to calculate its ratios [11, 24]. 
*   **Thresholds:** It capped debt and cash at **33%**, but was uniquely lenient on accounts receivable, allowing them to comprise up to **49%** of the 36-month average market capitalization [11, 24, 32]. 
*   *Recent Changes:* Mirroring DJIM, S&P also **removed its cash and receivables screening requirements in September 2023**, moving to a simplified leverage-and-revenue-only screening model [30, 31].


## 6.6 Islamic REITs and ETFs

**Islamic Real Estate Investment Trusts (REITs)**

Islamic REITs function similarly to conventional REITs by offering investors pooled access to real estate, but they are strictly governed by Shariah principles regarding their investments, tenants, and capital structures [1-3]. **Malaysia is the principal jurisdiction and global leader in establishing regulatory frameworks for Islamic REITs** [2, 3]. The Securities Commission Malaysia (SC) has issued comprehensive Guidelines for Islamic REITs that provide Shariah guidance on business activities, complementing the existing conventional REIT frameworks [4, 5].

**Shariah-Compliant Tenants and Mixed Activities**
A fundamental requirement for an Islamic REIT is that its tenants must operate permissible businesses. The REIT cannot lease space for *haram* (prohibited) activities, such as conventional interest-based banking, non-Takaful insurance, gambling, pornography, or the manufacture and sale of alcohol, tobacco, arms, and pork products [2, 3, 6, 7]. 

However, to accommodate commercial realities, **the SC's Shariah Advisory Council (SAC) allows Islamic REITs to acquire properties with tenants engaged in "mixed activities," provided the rental income from non-permissible activities does not exceed a 20% benchmark of the REIT's total turnover** [8, 9]. 
* If the non-permissible rental income exceeds 20%, the Shariah adviser must instruct the fund manager not to invest in that property [8].
* An Islamic REIT is strictly prohibited from acquiring a property where *all* tenants operate non-permissible activities (e.g., a casino building), even if the total rental income falls below the 20% threshold [10].
* For vacant properties seeking new tenants, the REIT cannot accept operators of fully non-permissible activities [11].
* To calculate the non-permissible rental portion for a tenant operating mixed activities (e.g., a supermarket selling both halal groceries and alcohol), the REIT can use the ratio of the physical floor area dedicated to the non-permissible activity against the total rented area [12, 13].

**Financing, Insurance, and Operations**
Islamic REITs must exclusively use **Shariah-compliant instruments for all investments, deposits, and financing** [14]. Conventional debt leverage is prohibited and must be replaced by Islamic financing facilities [15, 16]. Furthermore, **Islamic REITs must insure their real estate assets using Takaful (Islamic insurance) schemes**, though conventional insurance is temporarily permitted if appropriate Takaful coverage is unavailable [14]. For risk management, they are permitted to participate in forward foreign exchange sales or purchases, ideally utilizing the Islamic concept of *wa'd* (promise) with Islamic financial institutions [17]. A practical example of a Malaysian Islamic REIT is the Al-Salam REIT, which holds Shariah-compliant assets such as QSR Brands (KFC and Pizza Hut) properties, retail malls, and office buildings [18].

**Islamic Exchange-Traded Funds (ETFs) and the iShares Sukuk UCITS ETF**

Islamic ETFs are passively managed open-ended funds that track Shariah-compliant indices, providing investors with a cost-effective, transparent, and diversified way to access halal equities and fixed-income alternatives [19-21]. 

A prominent example in the fixed-income space is the **iShares $ Sukuk UCITS ETF (ticker: SKUK)**, managed by BlackRock [22, 23].
* **Investment Objective:** The fund aims to reflect the total return performance of the **J.P. Morgan EM Aggregate Sukuk Index** [23, 24].
* **Asset Composition:** It measures the performance of US Dollar-denominated emerging market Sukuk issued by sovereign, quasi-sovereign, corporate, and supranational entities [24].
* **Shariah Compliance:** The tracked index strictly includes instruments labeled as Sukuk that comply with the standards set by the Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI) [24].
* **Structure:** It is a physical, sampled ETF domiciled in Ireland, with its base currency in USD [23, 25].

**Challenges in the Islamic ETF Market**
While Islamic ETFs like the iShares Sukuk UCITS ETF offer vital market access, they face unique structural hurdles. **Passive Sukuk funds and ETFs often carry higher management fees than their conventional counterparts** [26]. This is driven by the additional costs of annual Shariah board audits, continuous compliance screening, and the requirement to identify and "purify" (donate to charity) any incidental non-compliant income [27, 28]. Additionally, because many Sukuk investors adopt a "buy-and-hold" strategy, secondary market liquidity is often low. This can increase trading costs and make it challenging for ETF managers to perfectly replicate index benchmarks without experiencing price volatility [26].


## 6.7 ESG and Sustainable Sukuk

The convergence of Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) criteria with Islamic finance is driven by a profound natural synergy between sustainable investing and *Maqasid al-Shariah* (the objectives of Islamic law) [1, 2]. Islamic finance inherently promotes ethical conduct, social justice, and environmental stewardship, viewing humans as trustees (*khalifah*) of the Earth [2, 3]. It relies on risk-sharing, asset-backed transactions, and positive societal impact (*maslahah*), while strictly prohibiting *riba* (interest), *gharar* (excessive uncertainty), *maysir* (speculation), and investments in harmful sectors [4-7]. This dual emphasis on avoiding harm through negative screening and promoting sustainable economic activity aligns seamlessly with global ESG mandates [2, 7, 8].

Driven by this alignment and the urgent need for climate finance, the sustainable sukuk market has experienced record growth. In 2024, **global issuances of ESG sukuk reached US$ 15.4 billion**, representing a 14.7% increase from the previous year and pushing the total outstanding value of ESG sukuk past the US$ 50 billion milestone [9, 10]. Green sukuk issuances alone exceeded USD 15 billion in 2024, heavily bolstered by commitments from GCC countries to fund energy transition targets, green infrastructure, and net-zero initiatives [9, 11, 12]. 

To standardize this rapid expansion and foster international trust, the International Capital Market Association (ICMA), the Islamic Development Bank (IsDB), and the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) jointly published the **"Guidance on Green, Social and Sustainability Sukuk"** in April 2024 [13-15]. This landmark guidance bridges sustainable finance conventions with Islamic structuring, confirming the wide applicability of the ICMA Principles (such as the Green Bond Principles and Sustainability Bond Guidelines) to the global sukuk market [16, 17]. The framework helps issuers appropriately label their sukuk, navigate the complex dual-approval issuance process, and align with international ESG standards, ultimately making sustainable sukuk more accessible to conventional ESG investors who prioritize sustainability over strict Shariah compliance [17-20].

Malaysia has served as a pioneering force in this ecosystem, having introduced the Sustainable and Responsible Investment (SRI) Sukuk Framework through its Securities Commission (SC) in 2014 [21]. Under this framework, **proceeds must be applied exclusively to eligible SRI projects**, which encompass green initiatives (like renewable energy and energy efficiency), social projects (like affordable housing and healthcare), and *waqf* (endowment) asset development [22-24]. Furthermore, the SC has established advanced requirements for **SRI-linked sukuk**, an innovative structure where the financial or structural characteristics of the sukuk (such as the profit rate) vary dynamically depending on whether the issuer meets predefined Sustainability Performance Targets (SPTs) measured against Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) [25-28]. Due to this highly supportive regulatory scaffolding, SRI sukuk issuances in Malaysia have surged nearly ninefold since their inception, reaching RM 11.9 billion in 2024 [29].

Despite the tremendous growth and synergies, the convergence of these two financial systems introduces the **dual risk of "greenwashing" and "Shariah-washing"** [30, 31]. Greenwashing occurs when the environmental or social benefits of the funded projects are overstated, lack credible KPIs, or fail to deliver the expected impact [30, 32]. Shariah-washing arises when sukuk mimic conventional debt in their economic substance—such as relying on binding purchase undertakings that guarantee the principal and eliminate true profit-and-loss sharing—despite bearing a superficial "Shariah-compliant" legal form [33-35]. To mitigate these intersecting reputational risks, market participants must ensure rigorous compliance through transparent use-of-proceeds reporting, credible external reviews (Second Party Opinions), and robust, independent Shariah governance mechanisms that verify both the ethical impact and the jurisprudential validity of the instruments [30, 31, 36, 37].
