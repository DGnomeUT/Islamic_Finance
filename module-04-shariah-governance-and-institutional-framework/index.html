    Module 4: Shariah Governance and Institutional Framework¶

    4.1 The Multi-Tier Governance Model¶

    The three-tier Shariah governance model is a comprehensive, multi-layered framework designed to ensure that Islamic Financial Institutions (IFIs) and their products strictly comply with Shariah principles—such as the prohibition of interest (riba), excessive uncertainty (gharar), and speculation (maysir). This structure balances the need for industry-wide standardization with institutional innovation, operating across macro, meso, and micro levels.

    Tier 1: National Authority (Macro-Level Oversight)¶

    The first tier consists of a centralized, national-level Shariah authority established by a country's financial regulator or central bank. This tier is designed to harmonize Shariah interpretations, provide the highest level of market certainty, and prevent "fatwa shopping" (where institutions seek out the most lenient rulings).

    • Malaysia (BNM SAC): Malaysia operates a highly centralized governance model. The Shariah Advisory Council (SAC) of Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM) acts as the sole authoritative body on Shariah matters for Islamic banking, finance, and takaful. Rulings issued by the SAC are legally binding not only on all IFIs but also on civil courts and arbitrators adjudicating Islamic finance disputes. A parallel SAC under the Securities Commission (SC) governs the Islamic capital markets.
    • UAE (HSA): The United Arab Emirates relies on the Higher Shari'ah Authority (HSA) at the Central Bank of the UAE. The HSA sets binding rules, standards, and general principles for the entire Islamic finance sector. It also approves the appointment of scholars to institutional-level Shariah boards and issues fatwas for major sovereign programs, such as the UAE Treasury Sukuk Programme.

    Tier 2: Institutional Shariah Board (Meso-Level Oversight)¶

    The second tier operates at the level of the individual bank, asset manager, or issuing entity. IFIs are required to appoint a Shariah Supervisory Board (SSB)—typically composed of three to five independent scholars specializing in Fiqh al-Muamalat (Islamic commercial jurisprudence).

    The Islamic Financial Services Board's IFSB-10 (Guiding Principles on Shariah Governance Systems) is the foundational global standard for this tier. It defines Shariah governance as a system ensuring effective independent oversight based on four core criteria: competence, independence, confidentiality, and consistency. The institutional SSB is responsible for: * Ex-Ante Approval: Reviewing product structures, legal documentation, and operational procedures before launch, and issuing a Fatwa (religious edict) certifying the product as halal. * Binding Authority: The SSB’s decisions are binding on the IFI’s management, meaning the institution cannot offer a product or proceed with an investment if the SSB deems it non-compliant. * Annual Reporting: Producing an annual Shariah compliance report that is presented to shareholders and supervisory authorities, detailing the institution's adherence to Shariah law over the financial year.

    Tier 3: Transaction-Level Review (Micro-Level Implementation)¶

    The third tier represents the internal control system, acting as the operational engine that ensures the SSB's rulings are accurately executed in day-to-day transactions. This tier utilizes a "lines of defense" model to mitigate Shariah non-compliance risk, which could otherwise lead to reputational damage or investor flight.

    • Internal Shariah Compliance Unit (ISCU): Acting as the second line of defense, this dedicated, independent unit translates the SSB's fatwas into operational guidelines. It disseminates these rules to operative personnel, reviews transactions before and after execution, and monitors daily compliance across the institution.
    • Internal Shariah Review/Audit (ISRU): Acting as the third line of defense, this function periodically audits actual financial contracts and transaction flows to verify compliance. If the ISRU identifies any non-compliant transactions, it reports them directly to the SSB or the Board's Audit Committee so the errors can be rectified.
    • Income Purification: At the transaction level, if any income is found to be tainted by non-permissible elements (e.g., accidental interest accumulation), the transaction review process dictates that this income must be stripped from the profit pool and donated to charity.

    By integrating national standardization (Tier 1), independent scholarly oversight (Tier 2), and rigorous internal audits (Tier 3), this governance architecture transforms Shariah compliance from a theoretical religious ideal into a highly regulated, defensible, and legally operational control system.

    4.2 AAOIFI and IFSB: The Global Standard Setters¶

    The Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI) Based in Bahrain and established in 1991, AAOIFI is the leading international standard-setting body responsible for developing Shariah, accounting, auditing, ethics, and governance standards for the global Islamic finance industry. AAOIFI's guidelines are mandatory in jurisdictions like Bahrain, Oman, and the UAE, and serve as influential guidelines in others, such as Malaysia and Saudi Arabia.

    • 62+ Standards: AAOIFI has issued numerous standards across multiple disciplines, currently boasting 62+ Shariah standards that govern the permissibility and mechanics of various Islamic financial transactions, ranging from Murabaha to Sukuk. These standards aim to harmonize practices globally, ensuring transparency and reducing inconsistencies across Shariah supervisory boards.
    • The Shariah Standard No. 62 (SS 62) Debate: The proposed Shariah Standard No. 62 focuses on Sukuk and has triggered high-level market debate regarding the tension between "form" (legal structure) and "substance" (Shariah economic objectives). SS 62 aims to reinforce Shariah authenticity by mandating the "true sale" and legal transfer of ownership of underlying assets to investors, thereby enforcing genuine risk-sharing (al-ghunm wa al-ghurm) and eliminating capital guarantees like fixed repurchase agreements.
    • Market Impact: While SS 62 enhances religious integrity, market participants and rating agencies have warned that a strict implementation could disrupt the market. Requiring true asset transfer introduces legal complexities, higher costs, and potential market fragmentation. If applied broadly, many existing asset-based Sukuk would need restructuring. Due to these concerns, the implementation of SS 62 has faced revisions and delays to allow the market to pragmatically adapt without compromising financial stability.

    The Islamic Financial Services Board (IFSB) Based in Kuala Lumpur and inaugurated in 2002, the IFSB focuses on the prudential regulation and supervision of the Islamic Financial Services Industry (IFSI).

    • Prudential and Governance Standards: While AAOIFI focuses heavily on Shariah and accounting rules, the IFSB adapts conventional regulatory frameworks (such as those from the Basel Committee and IOSCO) to accommodate the specific risk profiles of Islamic institutions. The IFSB issues standards on capital adequacy, risk management, and corporate governance. For example, the IFSB provides guidance on unique Islamic finance risks, such as Displaced Commercial Risk and the scarcity of High-Quality Liquid Assets (HQLA). Furthermore, IFSB-10 sets out guiding principles for Shariah Governance Systems, helping regulators ensure that institutions have effective, independent Shariah oversight.
    • IFSI Stability Report: The IFSB publishes the flagship Islamic Financial Services Industry Stability Report, which has become a vital benchmark for global stakeholders. The 2025 Stability Report highlighted that global IFSI assets grew by 14.9% year-on-year, reaching $3.88 trillion in 2024. Despite this robust growth and stable financial soundness indicators, the report underscored critical structural vulnerabilities. It noted that the industry is heavily concentrated in Islamic banking (which holds over 70% of assets) and warned that the underdevelopment of Islamic capital markets (Sukuk) and Islamic insurance (Takaful) limits financial intermediation, narrows liquidity management channels, and exposes the industry to external economic shocks.

    The International Islamic Financial Market (IIFM) Established in Bahrain in 2002, the IIFM is a neutral, non-profit standard-setting body dedicated to standardizing Shariah-compliant financial contracts and product templates for the capital, money market, corporate finance, and trade finance sectors.

    • ISDA/IIFM Tahawwut Master Agreement: One of the IIFM's most significant contributions is the development of the Tahawwut (Islamic hedging) Master Agreement, created in partnership with the International Swaps and Derivatives Association (ISDA). Before this agreement, Islamic hedging structures were bespoke, requiring lengthy, institution-specific bilateral negotiations.
    • Market Impact: The Tahawwut framework provides standardized documentation for complex derivatives, such as Islamic Profit Rate Swaps, cross-currency swaps, and FX forwards. This standardization is crucial because it reduces transaction costs, minimizes legal challenges, promotes transparency, and enhances secondary market liquidity. By providing globally accepted documentation, the IIFM enables Islamic financial institutions to effectively manage currency and rate-of-return exposures while remaining strictly compliant with Shariah principles.

    4.3 Jurisdictional Divergence¶

    The global Islamic finance industry has expanded into a multi-trillion-dollar ecosystem, driven by the need to structure capital in ways that avoid riba (interest), gharar (excessive uncertainty), and maysir (speculation) while linking returns to real economic activity. However, the global landscape exhibits significant jurisdictional divergence resulting from differing interpretations of Shariah (fiqh), varied legal traditions, and contrasting regulatory philosophies. Three distinct paradigms dominate the market today: the GCC's conservative and market-driven approach, Malaysia's highly codified and permissive hybrid system, and the UK's secular, tax-neutral structuring hub.

    The GCC: Conservative Scrutiny and Market-Driven Governance In the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC)—which hosts some of the world's largest Islamic banking assets—governance has historically been decentralized or "minimalist". Traditionally, institutions operated under self-regulation, relying on individual Shariah Supervisory Boards (SSBs) and prominent scholars to approve products, though there is a recent shift toward centralized authorities to harmonize rulings, such as the Higher Sharia Authority in the UAE and Kuwait's Higher Committee of Shariah Supervision.

    • Strict Adherence to Form and Authenticity: GCC scholars tend to favor strict interpretations of traditional Islamic contracts, prioritizing the legal form and theological authenticity of transactions over mere economic outcomes.
    • Tawarruq and Debt Restrictions: GCC regulators and scholars frequently scrutinize synthetic liquidity tools like tawarruq (commodity murabaha) and the trading of debt (bai al-dayn). While tawarruq is heavily used for treasury management, bodies like the OIC's International Islamic Fiqh Academy (IIFA) have issued rulings condemning "organized tawarruq" because its economic substance heavily mirrors interest-based lending without genuine asset transfer.
    • AAOIFI Alignment: The GCC closely aligns with the Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI). AAOIFI standards (such as Standard 59 on the Sale of Debt and Standard 62 for Sukuk) impose strict constraints on hybrid structures and demand true-sale tangibility of assets, expressly discouraging guaranteed fixed-price undertakings.

    Malaysia: A Codified Framework and Permissive Hybrids Malaysia operates an interventionist, pro-active regulatory model that integrates Islamic finance directly into a comprehensive national economic strategy. Guided by Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM) and the Securities Commission (SC), Malaysia employs centralized National Shariah Advisory Councils whose rulings are binding on both financial institutions and civil courts, effectively preventing "fatwa shopping".

    • Pragmatism and "Substance over Form": Rather than rigid adherence to classical contract mechanics, Malaysian scholars frequently rely on maslahah (public interest/benefit) to facilitate market liquidity and financial inclusion.
    • Embracing Hybrids and Liquidity Tools: This pragmatism creates a more permissive environment for hybrid financial instruments that might face fierce resistance in the Middle East. Malaysia widely permits the sale of debt (bai al-dayn), structured tawarruq for home and corporate financing, and musharakah mutanaqisa (diminishing partnership).
    • Independence from AAOIFI: While the BNM actively pushes for "Value-Based Intermediation" (VBI) to align finance with sustainable goals, the national framework retains structural flexibility. Malaysia consciously decides not to formally bind itself to the strict AAOIFI standards that govern GCC markets, allowing it to support the rapid growth of its massive sukuk market and Islamic fintech ecosystem without stalling innovation.

    The UK: The Secular Structuring Hub Unlike Muslim-majority jurisdictions, the UK manages Islamic finance in a secular, common-law environment without a dedicated Shariah regulatory layer. The UK's Financial Conduct Authority (FCA) treats Islamic and conventional institutions equally, declining to adjudicate religious compliance and leaving Shariah certification entirely to independent boards or the market.

    • Tax Neutrality and "Alternative Finance": To overcome regulatory obstacles like double stamp duty land tax on asset-backed transactions (e.g., ijarah), the UK government pioneered "Alternative Finance Arrangements" in its Finance Acts. By ensuring tax neutrality—where the profit markup in an Islamic transaction is treated equivalently to conventional interest for tax purposes—the UK successfully leveled the playing field.
    • Legal Enforceability: English law is chosen as the governing law for the majority of cross-border Islamic contracts globally. English courts have consistently ruled that commercial contract terms take precedence over retroactive claims of Shariah non-compliance (as seen in cases like Dana Gas and Beximco), providing the absolute legal certainty that international investors demand.
    • Global Hub Status: This robust legal architecture, alongside early sovereign sukuk issuances (in 2014 and 2021) and a centralized Bank of England Alternative Liquidity Facility (ALF), has cemented London as the premier Western hub for Islamic funds, sukuk listings, corporate structuring, and sustainable finance initiatives.

    4.4 Scholar Concentration and Governance Risks¶

    Scholar concentration risks stem from a severe global shortage of experts who possess deep knowledge of both Fiqh al-Muamalat (Islamic commercial jurisprudence) and complex modern finance. Consequently, a small group of "superstar" scholars sit on hundreds of Shariah Supervisory Boards (SSBs) globally, creating intense key-person risks, capacity bottlenecks, and an unsustainable workload. For context, around 20 Shariah scholars hold more than half of the market share, with the top three earning approximately US $4.5 million annually in fees. This overextension can lead to "rubber-stamping" complex financial structures rather than providing rigorous, dedicated oversight.

    Conflicts of interest naturally arise from this concentration and the industry's remuneration structures. Because Islamic financial institutions (IFIs) directly pay the scholars who regulate them, there is a powerful financial incentive for scholars to be excessively lenient in their certifications to retain lucrative board positions. Additionally, when a single scholar serves on the boards of multiple competing institutions concurrently, it creates significant risks regarding corporate confidentiality, insider knowledge, and divided loyalties.

    Insufficient independence further compounds these governance risks. While international guidelines, such as those from the Islamic Financial Services Board (IFSB), stress that scholars must exercise objective judgment free from management duress, true autonomy is often lacking. In many setups, the management that designs the financial products also appoints and compensates the SSB. Without stringent legal separation, transparent conflict-of-interest declarations, or mechanisms allowing scholars to bypass management and report directly to shareholders or regulators, SSBs may lack the structural power to challenge management's commercial objectives.

    Lack of standardised qualifications means there is no universally applied benchmark for who can serve as a Shariah advisor. While some jurisdictions, like Bahrain, enforce strict "fit and proper" criteria—requiring a bachelor's degree in Shariah, certification in Fiqh al-Muamalat, and adequate understanding of banking and accounting—many other regulatory regimes lack such mandates. As a result, boards frequently suffer from a "knowledge gap," where scholars with traditional religious training have limited understanding of modern financial engineering, making them ill-equipped to evaluate the economic substance of intricate instruments like derivatives or synthetic sukuk.

    Inconsistent audit practices weaken the ongoing enforcement of Islamic principles. While securing an initial Fatwa (religious edict) is standard practice, ex-post compliance—ensuring a product remains compliant throughout its lifecycle—is highly variable. Many regulatory regimes in the GCC and Southeast Asia do not mandate independent external Shariah audits. Although best practices advocate for a "three lines of defense" model featuring internal Shariah reviews and periodic external Shariah audits, the lack of standardized audit methodologies and a shortage of qualified external Shariah auditors severely compromises the industry's ability to detect and rectify non-compliance after a product launches.

    'Scholar shopping' (or Fatwa shopping) occurs when financial institutions exploit the fragmented nature of Shariah interpretation to find scholars willing to approve controversial products. Because there is no single global Shariah authority, an instrument rejected by one board might be approved by another. IFIs may clandestinely search for "collaborating" scholars who hold permissive views to endorse new, often highly conventional-looking instruments. This practice, heavily criticized as "Shariah arbitrage," erodes public trust and accelerates the proliferation of products that adhere to Islamic law in their legal form but mimic conventional, interest-based finance in their economic substance.

    4.5 Standardisation vs Innovation¶

    The Permanent Tension: Standardisation vs. Innovation (Form vs. Substance) The Islamic financial services industry, now valued at nearly $4 trillion globally, is defined by a permanent, structural tension between standardisation and innovation. At its core, this is a debate over "form versus substance". On one side, innovation is necessary to drive market expansion, facilitate cross-border competitiveness, and deploy new technologies like blockchain, smart contracts, and ESG-linked structures to meet modern commercial demands. On the other side, standardisation seeks to enforce strict adherence to the true spirit of Shariah—demanding genuine risk-sharing, material finality, and asset-backed investments rather than just legally rebranding conventional debt instruments.

    This dialectical friction is permanent because standardising purely for Shariah authenticity often curtails the flexibility needed to scale products and attract institutional capital, whereas unbridled innovation risks creating synthetic, debt-like replicas (such as organized Tawarruq) that dilute the industry's ethical and risk-sharing foundations.

    The AAOIFI Standard 62 Debate The recent controversy surrounding the Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI) Shariah Standard No. 62 perfectly encapsulates this conflict. Standard 62 aims to force the global Sukuk market back to its authentic roots by mandating a "true sale" and legal transfer of underlying assets from issuers to investors. It effectively prohibits the use of explicit capital guarantees and fixed-price purchase undertakings at nominal value, moving the market away from "asset-based" structures (which mimic unsecured conventional bonds) toward genuinely "asset-backed" risk-sharing partnerships.

    While Shariah purists welcome this move to eliminate "Shariah arbitrage," the draft standard has sparked widespread apprehension among issuers, bankers, and regulators. Industry practitioners warn that stripping away bond-like predictability introduces profound contractual complexity, disrupts established treasury operations, and risks alienating conventional institutional investors who rely on the fixed-income profile of traditional Sukuk. Due to intense pushback, particularly in jurisdictions like the UAE where AAOIFI compliance is mandatory, the standard's implementation has been delayed to undergo revisions for practical viability.

    Cost and Liquidity Impacts The push for stricter standardisation carries immense cost and liquidity implications for Islamic markets. Mandating authentic asset transfers elevates legal, auditing, and structural costs, requiring the establishment of specialized Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs) and intricate valuation of physical assets, which can make Sukuk issuance prohibitively expensive for corporates and SMEs.

    Furthermore, Islamic banks fundamentally suffer from a scarcity of Shariah-compliant high-quality liquid assets (HQLA) and lack access to conventional interbank money markets, forcing heavy reliance on cash buffers or controversial mechanisms like Commodity Murabaha. Enforcing strict asset-backed models alters the risk profile of these instruments, prompting investors to adopt a "buy-and-hold" strategy that drastically shrinks the supply of tradable assets, widens bid-ask spreads, and chokes secondary market liquidity. While institutions like the International Islamic Liquidity Management Corporation (IILM) provide highly-rated, short-term Sukuk to ease these bottlenecks, structural and cross-border liquidity constraints will persist if standardisation rules become overly restrictive.

    Rating Agency Warnings and Market Fragmentation Major rating agencies, including Fitch, Moody's, and S&P, have explicitly warned that transitioning away from predictable, unsecured debt-like structures to pure risk-sharing could destabilize the market. If standardisation removes the explicit purchase undertakings that historically guaranteed investor principal, the credit profile of a Sukuk will rely solely on the underlying asset's performance rather than the sovereign or corporate issuer's credit rating. Rating agencies caution that such instruments might face severe downgrades or become entirely "unrateable" under conventional fixed-income methodologies, causing a bifurcated market that institutional capital may avoid.

    Additionally, these agencies emphasize the severe risk of market fragmentation if regulatory adoption is inconsistent. Because Islamic finance lacks a single, centralized global authority, varying interpretations will arise; for example, GCC nations might rigidly enforce AAOIFI rules, while massive markets like Malaysia maintain their independent, more flexible regulatory frameworks. This disharmony threatens to segment the global investor base, invite regulatory arbitrage, and disrupt the seamless flow of cross-border capital.

    Conclusion The tension between the ideological mandates of Islamic jurisprudence and the pragmatic realities of global capital markets is a permanent feature of the industry. Navigating it requires a calibrated, phased approach to regulatory changes—like Standard 62—so that markets can adjust without liquidity shocks. Moving forward, leveraging technological innovations such as blockchain and smart contracts can help automate compliance, increase transparency, and bridge the gap, allowing the industry to honor its authentic risk-sharing substance while maintaining the operational efficiency needed to scale globally

    4.6 The Fatwa Process¶

    Definition of a Fatwa in Islamic Finance In the context of Islamic finance, a fatwa (plural: fatawa) is a juristic opinion, religious edict, or pronouncement on a matter pertaining to Shariah issues, typically issued by a recognized scholar or a Shariah Supervisory Board (SSB). When an Islamic Financial Institution (IFI) decides to implement a fatwa regarding its operations or products, it assumes the status of a binding Shariah ruling (hukm al-Shar’i) with full legal effect for that institution.

    The Typical Fatwa Process The Shariah governance process for issuing and maintaining a fatwa involves a comprehensive ex-ante (pre-launch) and ex-post (post-launch) framework.

    • Product Review: During the design and development phase, the product team presents the proposed financial structure to the SSB. The scholars rigorously scrutinize the legal contracts, cash flows, investment criteria, and operational documents to ensure the transaction avoids prohibited elements such as riba (interest), gharar (excessive uncertainty), and maysir (gambling).
    • Conditional Approval: After the initial review, the SSB often grants an "in principle" or conditional approval of the structure paper. The board may request structural modifications before the final launch. Once all Shariah requirements are met in the final documentation, a formal fatwa is issued certifying the product as Shariah-compliant.
    • Ongoing Monitoring and Audit: The fatwa process does not end at product launch. The SSB—supported by internal Shariah compliance officers and independent Shariah auditors—conducts continuous monitoring and periodic audits. This ensures that the day-to-day execution of the contracts strictly adheres to the fatwa. If any deviations are found, the SSB mandates corrective actions, such as purifying non-compliant income by donating it to charity.

    Fatwa Quality and Product Defensibility The rigor, independence, and transparency of the fatwa process directly dictate a financial product's market credibility, legal certainty, and overall defensibility.

    • Ensuring Economic Substance: A high-quality fatwa ensures that a product possesses true economic substance and risk-sharing mechanisms, rather than merely acting as an "Islamic wrapper" that synthetically replicates conventional interest-bearing debt. Products that withstand the "five-question robustness test" (which evaluates asset linkage, risk-sharing, substance over form, enforceability, and scholarly consensus) are highly defensible in the market.
    • Mitigating Reputational and Legal Risk: Ambiguity concerning a product's Shariah compliance can severely damage an institution's reputation and lead to "fund flight," where religious investors abruptly withdraw their capital. A rigorous fatwa process safeguards against this by ensuring the product can withstand public and regulatory scrutiny.
    • Preventing "Fatwa Shopping": Poor fatwa quality often stems from "fatwa shopping"—a clandestine practice where institutions seek out lenient scholars willing to endorse controversial products. This practice compromises the ethical integrity of Islamic finance and erodes consumer trust. Robust governance frameworks, standardized rulings, and independent boards are required to prevent this and ensure that fatwas serve as reliable, defensible pillars for global capital allocation.

    Executive Refresher

    - **The Three-Tier Governance Architecture:** Shariah governance operates at three levels — the institutional SSB (transaction-level certification), the national Shariah authority (e.g., Bank Negara Malaysia's SAC or SECP's Shariah advisory committee), and international standard-setters (AAOIFI for standards, IFSB for prudential guidelines). Understanding which tier a ruling comes from — and its binding force — is essential for cross-border deal structuring. - **SSB Independence is a Critical Due Diligence Factor:** A Shariah supervisory board is only as robust as the independence of its scholars. Key risk factors include scholars sitting on the SSBs of both counterparties in a transaction, boards dominated by a single scholar without peer review, and SSB fees contingent on transaction approval. When conducting due diligence on a counterparty's Shariah compliance, always review SSB composition, mandate, and remuneration structure. - **AAOIFI Standards Are Not Universally Binding:** AAOIFI Shariah Standards are adopted in varying degrees — mandatory in Bahrain, UAE, Qatar, and Sudan, but treated as guidelines (alongside local SSB fatwas) in Malaysia and Pakistan. This creates cross-border structuring risk: a deal approved under GCC standards may face challenge if executed using Malaysian governing law. Always confirm which standard set applies before finalising documentation. - **The Centralised vs Decentralised Debate Has Real Commercial Consequences:** Malaysia's centralised model (SAC rulings are binding on all institutions) provides certainty but limits product innovation at the institutional level. GCC jurisdictions' decentralised model allows more flexibility but creates inconsistency and 'Shariah shopping' risk. When advising on jurisdiction selection for an Islamic structure, the governance model is as important a factor as tax treatment or legal enforceability. - **Shariah Audit is Distinct from Shariah Review:** A Shariah review (pre-transaction) certifies that a proposed structure meets Shariah requirements. A Shariah audit (post-execution) verifies that the approved structure was actually implemented as certified. Both are required under AAOIFI Governance Standard 2. Skipping the post-execution audit is a common institutional weakness — rectifiable findings at audit stage can require unwinding transactions or compensatory charitable payments (Gharamah).
