    Module 1: Foundations of Islamic Finance¶

    1.1 Core Philosophy and Operating Principles¶

    Islamic finance is a distinct, constraint-driven financial system governed by Shariah (Islamic law), rather than simply being conventional finance without interest. It is grounded in an ontological view of wealth and commerce that seeks to align economic activity with moral responsibility, social justice, and transparency.

    Shariah Derivation The rules governing Islamic finance are derived from Fiqh al-Muamalat (Islamic jurisprudence of transactions). This legal framework is drawn from four primary sources: * The Quran: The holy book of divine guidance. * The Sunnah (or Hadith): The recorded sayings, practices, and traditions of the Prophet Muhammad. * Ijma (Scholarly Consensus): The unanimous agreement of Islamic scholars on a specific issue. * Qiyas and Ijtihad (Analogy and Legal Reasoning): The process of analogical deduction and personal effort by qualified scholars to apply established principles to modern, unprecedented financial situations.

    Not Merely "Interest-Free Banking" A common misconception is that Islamic finance simply removes the interest component from conventional banking. However, it is fundamentally a different paradigm. In conventional finance, money is treated as a commodity that can be rented out for a price (interest). In Islamic finance, money is viewed strictly as a measure of value and a medium of exchange with no intrinsic utility. Consequently, money cannot "breed" more money simply through the passage of time.

    To earn a legitimate return, capital must be transformed into an asset or service. Profit is legitimized only through the assumption of commercial risk and effort (Al-Ghunm bi al-Ghurm—profit follows the risk of loss). This requires valid asset ownership (milk) or possession, shifting the traditional creditor-debtor relationship into one of buyer-seller, lessor-lessee, or joint partners. By avoiding zero-sum speculation, the system directs capital away from a "casino economy" and channels it into real, productive ventures.

    The Two-Component Framework: Prohibitions and Positive Requirements The easiest way to make Islamic finance "computable" and structurally understandable is to view it as an operating system governed by two components: things you cannot do (prohibitions) and things you must do (positive requirements).

    (i) The Prohibitions (What you cannot do) * Riba (Interest/Usury): The absolute prohibition of earning a predetermined, guaranteed return purely from lending money. Riba is considered exploitative because it favors the lender at the expense of the borrower, allowing wealth accumulation without risk or effort. * Gharar (Excessive Uncertainty/Ambiguity): Contracts must be entirely clear regarding their subject matter, delivery, price, and terms. This prevents deceit and exploitation through information asymmetry, invalidating conventional derivatives (like options and futures) or short-selling where the underlying asset is unknown or unowned. * Maysir and Qimar (Gambling/Speculation): Transactions where wealth transfer relies on chance rather than productive economic effort are prohibited. This ensures that returns are derived from value creation rather than speculative betting. * Haram (Illicit) Activities: Capital cannot be deployed into industries that contradict Islamic ethical values. This requires negative screening to exclude sectors like alcohol, pork production, gambling, adult entertainment, weapons, and conventional interest-based finance.

    (ii) The Positive Requirements (What you must do) * Promotion of Risk-Sharing (Profit and Loss Sharing - PLS): Financial relationships should be structured so that the provider of capital and the entrepreneur share both the financial risks and the rewards of a business venture (e.g., through Mudarabah or Musharakah contracts). This aligns incentives and prevents the lender from monopolizing the economy. * Linkage to Real Productive Activity (Asset-Backing): Every financial transaction must be directly tied to tangible, identifiable assets or real economic services. This ensures that finance serves the real economy and limits artificial credit expansion and "financialization". * Ownership and Risk Transfer: A party cannot earn a profit from an asset unless they bear the related commercial risks (even if briefly), dictating strict rules on title transfer and possession. * Ethical, Social, and Economic Justice: The ultimate objective (Maqasid al-Shariah) is to promote societal welfare, protect human self and intellect, and ensure the equitable distribution of wealth. This is practically enforced through mechanisms like Zakat (mandatory charitable almsgiving) and Qard Hasan (benevolent, interest-free loans for those in need), ensuring that wealth circulates to benefit the broader community.

    1.2 The Three Core Prohibitions¶

    Riba (Usury and Interest) In Islamic finance, Riba literally translates to "excess," "increase," or "addition". Precisely defined under Shariah terminology, it refers to any predetermined, guaranteed excess or premium charged on a loan of money or fungible goods without any due consideration or effort provided in return. The prohibition of Riba is universally accepted and unanimously agreed upon by all major schools of Islamic jurisprudence, serving as the most consequential constraint in Islamic structuring.

    Conventional interest-bearing loans and corporate bonds are strictly excluded from Islamic finance because they treat money as a commodity that can be rented out for a price over time. Shariah views money merely as a medium of exchange and a measure of value with no intrinsic utility. Therefore, generating "money-on-money rent" purely from the passage of time is prohibited. For a return to be permissible, the capital provider must bear the commercial risk of the venture. In conventional lending, lenders secure an unjust advantage by guaranteeing their profits and shielding themselves from the economic risks of the underlying business, which violates the core Islamic principles of equity, justice, and profit-and-loss sharing.

    Gharar (Excessive Uncertainty) Gharar refers to excessive uncertainty, ambiguity, hazard, or peril within the fundamental terms of a contract. Specifically, it denotes situations where essential elements such as the subject matter, the price, the exact time of delivery, or the ultimate outcome are unknown or opaque to the contracting parties.

    Islamic jurisprudence distinguishes between major (excessive) gharar and minor gharar. Major gharar completely invalidates a contract because it can lead to exploitation, unfair advantages, and future disputes. Conversely, minor gharar is tolerated as a necessary evil; it is permitted if the uncertainty is trivial, unavoidable in normal commercial dealings, or if the overall public benefit of the transaction outweighs the minor risks involved.

    A direct application of the prohibition of major gharar is the strict rule against selling what you do not own (such as short-selling). This is prohibited because it involves selling an asset whose future existence, characteristics, and deliverability are uncertain at the time of the contract. If a seller does not possess the asset, they cannot guarantee delivery, introducing a high level of hazard and deception that invalidates the sale.

    Maysir (Gambling and Speculation) Maysir strictly prohibits gambling and highly speculative, chance-based contracts. It is defined as an agreement where the acquisition of wealth is entirely dependent on chance, unpredictable outcomes, or the sheer luck of a future event. In such zero-sum transactions, one party's gain is directly derived from the other party's loss, without any underlying productive economic activity.

    Islamic finance carefully distinguishes Maysir from productive risk-taking. While all legitimate business involves commercial risk, Islamic principles strongly encourage "commercial risk" where profits are justified by capital investment, active labor, and shared effort in real economic ventures. Speculative risk (Maysir), however, decouples finance from the real economy, creating synthetic wealth through bets on random events (like naked options or pure derivatives) and is therefore forbidden.

    Halal/Haram Screening To ensure full Shariah compliance, Islamic finance employs a strict two-stage screening architecture for investments, particularly for listed equities:

    1. Prohibited Activities (Qualitative Screening): Islamic funds and portfolios are strictly forbidden from investing in companies whose core business activities conflict with Islamic ethical values. Prohibited (Haram) sectors universally include conventional financial intermediation (interest-based banks and insurance), alcohol, tobacco, pork-related products, gambling/casinos, weapons and defense, and adult entertainment.

    2. Financial-Ratio-Level Screening (Quantitative Screening): Because most modern companies interact with conventional banking in some capacity, index providers (such as AAOIFI, MSCI, S&P, and FTSE) apply strict financial ratios to limit a permissible company's exposure to interest and debt. While exact thresholds vary slightly by standard-setting bodies, they generally mandate that:

    3. Interest-bearing debt must not exceed 30% to 33.33% of a company's total assets or trailing 12-month average market capitalization.
    4. Cash and interest-bearing securities must also stay below the 30% to 33.33% threshold of total assets or market cap.
    5. Accounts receivable typically must not exceed 33.33% to 49% of total assets or market cap, limiting the trading of pure debt.
    6. Impure Income (revenue generated from incidental non-permissible activities or interest) must be severely restricted, universally capped at a maximum of 5% of total revenue. Any impure income that is earned must subsequently be "purified" by being donated to charity.

    1.3 Positive Structuring Principles¶

    1. Real economy linkage — returns tied to trade/lease/investment In Islamic finance, money is viewed solely as a medium of exchange and a measure of value, possessing no intrinsic utility on its own. Therefore, wealth cannot be generated through the mere passage of time or by lending money for interest. Instead, the principle of material finality requires that every financial transaction be directly linked to a tangible underlying asset, usufruct (the right to use an asset), or a legitimate real economic activity. To earn a legitimate return, capital must be deployed through permitted channels: selling an owned asset, leasing an owned asset, or investing as a partner in a productive venture. This asset-backed nature intentionally tethers the financial sector to the real economy, avoiding the decoupling, excessive leveraging, and speculation often seen in conventional finance.

    2. Ownership and risk transfer — profit requires bearing related risk, rules on possession (qabd), title, AAOIFI Shariah Standard 8 A foundational maxim of Islamic jurisprudence is al-ghunm wa al-ghurm, which dictates that the entitlement to profit or reward is justified only by the assumption of commensurate commercial risk. A party cannot earn a return on an asset unless it bears the associated risks of ownership, even if only briefly. This drives strict rules regarding title and possession (qabd), forbidding the sale of assets one does not actually own or possess. AAOIFI Shariah Standard 8, which governs Murabaha (cost-plus sale) transactions, explicitly mandates that an institution must acquire actual or constructive possession of an asset—and thereby assume the risk of its destruction or impairment—before it can legally sell that asset to a customer. This ensures that the transaction is a genuine trade transferring real risk, rather than a camouflaged interest-bearing loan.

    3. Fair dealing and clarity — clear terms, no hidden leverage To ensure socioeconomic justice and prevent exploitation, Islamic finance strictly prohibits Gharar (excessive uncertainty or ambiguity) and Maysir (gambling and pure speculation). Financial contracts must be characterized by absolute clarity and transparency regarding the subject matter, the price, the delivery timeline, and the respective rights and obligations of all parties. Because of the ban on excessive uncertainty and the requirement that assets actually exist and be owned, complex conventional derivatives (like naked options, futures, and credit default swaps), short selling, and hidden synthetic leverage are generally prohibited. These rules are designed to protect weaker parties from hidden risks, prevent unjust advantages, and ensure that all transactions are built on clear mutual consent.

    4. Debt vs trade vs partnership — pure debt only as Qard Hasan or trade context Under Islamic law, lending money to generate a guaranteed, predetermined return is considered Riba (usury) and is strictly forbidden. Consequently, pure debt is only permissible as a Qard Hasan (a benevolent loan), which is an interest-free loan where the borrower is only obligated to repay the principal amount, often utilized for charitable, welfare, or temporary liquidity purposes.

    Because pure interest-based lending is banned, all other debt must organically arise within a trade context. Debt-creating instruments, such as Murabaha (mark-up sale) or Ijarah (leasing), generate a debt obligation because a tangible asset is sold or leased on a deferred payment basis, not because cash is lent for cash. Alternatively, institutions utilize partnership-based (equity-creating) structures like Mudarabah (trustee finance) and Musharakah (joint venture), where capital providers and entrepreneurs pool resources, share profits according to pre-agreed ratios, and bear financial losses strictly in proportion to their capital contributions.

    1.4 Asset Linkage and Commercial Legitimacy¶

    In Islamic finance, the concept of asset linkage mandates that all financial transactions must be intrinsically tied to real, identifiable economic activities or tangible assets, ensuring that profit is generated through trade, lease, or genuine investment rather than the mere passage of time. "Asset-backing" requires far more than nominally referencing an asset in a prospectus; it necessitates that a transaction utilizes identifiable assets and clearly defines how legal rights and commercial risk flows are allocated between parties. This principle ensures that money functions solely as a medium of exchange to support productive economic ventures, rather than acting as a commodity that generates profit from pure capital.

    The application of asset linkage has led to a critical structural distinction in the capital markets between asset-based and asset-backed sukuk (Islamic investment certificates): * Asset-Based Structures: Making up the vast majority of the market, asset-based sukuk do not involve a legal "true sale" of the underlying assets to investors. Instead, investors only hold a beneficial ownership interest, and the assets generally remain on the originator's balance sheet. Investors rely primarily on the general creditworthiness of the obligor rather than the performance of the assets themselves. This credit reliance is typically enforced through a binding "purchase undertaking," wherein the issuer promises to buy back the assets at face value upon maturity or an event of default, effectively guaranteeing the principal and mirroring the risk profile of conventional unsecured corporate debt. * Asset-Backed Structures: In contrast, true asset-backed structures involve a legally enforceable "true sale" of the assets to a bankruptcy-remote Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV). In this model, investors have strict recourse to the underlying assets and no claim against the originator's general credit. Consequently, these structures embody genuine risk-sharing; if the underlying asset underperforms, is impaired, or is destroyed, the investors directly absorb the financial loss.

    To manage liquidity and working capital, the industry also heavily utilizes debt-like hybrid instruments, most notably commodity murabaha (also known as organized tawarruq). In this multi-step arrangement, a financier purchases a highly liquid, freely tradable commodity (such as metals on the London Metal Exchange) and sells it to the client at a marked-up deferred price; the client then immediately sells the commodity on the spot market to a third party to obtain cash. While commodity murabaha formally utilizes Shariah-compliant trade contracts, its economic substance is highly synthetic and closely replicates a conventional interest-bearing loan or revolving credit facility.

    The increasing dominance of these debt-like hybrid instruments has drawn significant regulatory scrutiny. According to the Islamic Financial Services Board (IFSB) Stability Report 2025, the heavy reliance on instruments like commodity murabaha introduces critical microprudential and macroprudential vulnerabilities into the Islamic financial system. At the micro level, these instruments create fixed-payment debt obligations that elevate leverage and produce balance sheet structures highly sensitive to macroeconomic shocks, much like conventional banks. At the macro level, utilizing such structures for short-term funding and interbank liquidity management amplifies systemic interconnectedness and potential contagion channels.

    The IFSB warns that these hybrid instruments fundamentally challenge the authentic risk-sharing principles of Islamic finance. To mitigate these structural imbalances and ensure long-term stability, the IFSB emphasizes the urgent need for coordinated policy action to deepen authentic Islamic capital markets, standardize sukuk structures, and develop genuine Shariah-compliant liquidity alternatives.

    1.5 Practical Commercial Application¶

    Practical Commercial Application for Senior Executives

    For senior executives, navigating Islamic finance requires a fundamental paradigm shift: Islamic finance is not merely "interest-free conventional finance," but rather a distinct, asset-centric model of commerce. In this system, money is viewed strictly as a medium of exchange, meaning it cannot "breed" more money merely through the passage of time. Profit must be legitimized by the assumption of commercial risk, the provision of a service, or the ownership of an asset.

    Practically, this means that "financial engineering" in the Islamic space revolves around creating valid sales, leases, partnerships, and agency agreements. If your organization is financing a fleet of aircraft or a real estate project, you are not "lending" money; you are "buying" the asset and "leasing" it (Ijara) or "selling" it at a markup (Murabaha). This shift profoundly changes the balance sheet treatment, tax implications, and the legal recovery process during an insolvency.

    Furthermore, Islamic structuring offers significant strategic advantages. It provides a natural overlap with ESG and sustainability mandates through its ethical screening processes, allowing for broader capital appeal. It also enables organizations to diversify their funding sources and access vast, underserved Muslim-majority investor bases. However, executives must rigorously stress-test these structures to ensure genuine economic substance, risk allocation, and true ownership transfer, as structures that merely mimic conventional debt cosmetically face severe reputational and Shariah non-compliance risks.

    Redesigning a Conventional Loan as Murabaha or Ijara

    A conventional loan relies on a simple mechanism: the lender provides cash to the borrower, and the borrower promises to repay the cash plus a guaranteed return (interest). This is viewed as "rent on money," which falls under the strict prohibition of riba (usury). To achieve a similar economic outcome—such as providing debt-like, fixed-rate financing—an executive can redesign the transaction using Shariah-compliant sales or leases.

    1. Redesigning via Murabaha (Cost-Plus Sale): Instead of advancing cash, the financial institution acts as a trader. * The Mechanism: The customer identifies an asset they need. The bank purchases the asset from a third-party supplier, taking legal ownership and bearing the associated risks (like damage or destruction) while it holds title. The bank then immediately sells the asset to the customer at a pre-agreed, disclosed price that includes the original cost plus a profit markup. * The Commercial Outcome: The customer repays the bank in deferred installments. The bank earns a fixed "trade profit" rather than interest. Because the transaction is anchored to a real asset and the bank temporarily assumed ownership risk, the profit is perfectly legitimate.

    2. Redesigning via Ijara (Leasing): Instead of lending money to buy an asset, the bank retains ownership of the asset and rents out its use (usufruct). * The Mechanism: The bank acquires the desired asset (e.g., machinery, real estate) and leases it to the customer for a specified period against agreed-upon rental payments. Crucially, the bank, as the legal owner, retains the core risks of ownership, such as the obligation for major maintenance and insurance, and bears the loss if the asset is completely destroyed without the lessee's fault. * The Commercial Outcome: The bank receives a steady stream of rental income (which can be benchmarked to floating rates like LIBOR). At the end of the lease term, ownership can be transferred to the customer through a separate promise or sale, a structure known as Ijara Muntahia Bittamleek (lease-to-own).

    The Four Islamic Structuring Primitives

    To execute these transactions, Islamic finance relies on a toolkit of core structuring primitives. Instead of the conventional model of "Lender cash -> Borrower promise -> Lender cash + interest," Islamic finance generates returns through four distinct commercial pathways:

    • (1) Sale — Trade Profit: (Buy asset -> Sell asset at a markup). In contracts like Murabaha (spot delivery, deferred payment), Salam (spot payment, deferred delivery of fungible goods), or Istisna (commissioned manufacturing), the financier generates a profit through trading. The return is legitimized by the financier taking temporary ownership and pricing the goods to include a commercial margin.
    • (2) Lease — Rental Income: (Own asset -> Lease usufruct for rent). In an Ijara contract, the financier owns an underlying physical asset and charges a fee for its use. The income is justified because the financier bears the continuous liabilities of ownership, such as depreciation, major repairs, and total loss.
    • (3) Partner — P&L Share: (Contribute capital -> Share profit/loss). In equity-based contracts like Musharaka (joint venture) or Mudaraba (silent partnership), the financier acts as an investor. Returns are never guaranteed; instead, profits are distributed according to a pre-agreed ratio based on the venture's actual performance. If the business fails, financial losses are borne strictly by the capital providers.
    • (4) Agency — Service Income: (Appoint agent -> Fee + optional incentive). In a Wakala contract, a principal appoints an agent to perform a specific task, such as managing an investment portfolio. The agent does not share in the capital risk but earns a predetermined agency fee (Ujrah) for their services, and can often keep any profits generated above a targeted benchmark as a performance incentive.

    1.6 Risks and Limitations¶

    Over-reliance on debt-like structures (Tawarruq) A fundamental premise of Islamic finance is to replace interest-based lending with genuine risk-sharing and asset-backed investments, such as equity partnerships (Musharakah and Mudarabah). In practice, however, the industry suffers from a "Murabaha Syndrome," exhibiting a disproportionate reliance on debt-like, fixed-return instruments to mitigate the agency problems, high monitoring costs, and information asymmetries inherent in profit-and-loss sharing.

    The most controversial of these is organized Tawarruq (commodity murabaha). Designed to generate cash liquidity, Tawarruq uses a "triangular" trade: a financier buys a commodity, sells it to the client at a markup on deferred payment, and the client (or an agent) immediately sells it to a third party for spot cash. While it meets institutional liquidity needs, it economically synthesizes an interest-bearing cash loan without any genuine intention to trade or utilize the underlying asset. Because it detaches finance from real economic productivity and can generate inverted pyramids of debt, the OIC Fiqh Academy and numerous scholars have condemned organized Tawarruq as a legal stratagem (hilah) that violates the spirit of Shariah.

    Jurisdictional divergence (Malaysia vs GCC) The absence of a single global Shariah authority has led to deep regulatory fragmentation, primarily split between Southeast Asia and the Middle East.

    Malaysia utilizes a highly centralized, interventionist model. The Bank Negara Malaysia (BNM) and the Securities Commission maintain National Shariah Advisory Councils whose rulings are binding on all institutions and courts, providing a high degree of legal certainty and preventing "fatwa shopping". Philosophically, Malaysian scholars often lean toward public benefit (maslahah) and economic substance, allowing for practices that the Gulf prohibits, such as the trading of debt (bai al-dayn) and the issuance of sukuk backed 100% by receivables.

    The GCC region historically operated on a decentralized, self-regulatory model where individual banks relied on their own Shariah boards, although countries like the UAE and Bahrain are now moving toward centralized authorities. The GCC generally adheres to stricter, more conservative interpretations of Shariah, heavily influenced by Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI) standards.

    This jurisdictional divergence means that a product approved in Kuala Lumpur might be rejected as non-compliant in Riyadh. This fragmentation significantly increases the cost of capital, complicates cross-border syndication, and forces multinational issuers to structure deals to the "lowest common denominator" to attract global liquidity.

    Operational complexity as the cost of compliance Because Islamic finance strictly forbids earning a return merely from the time value of money, capital must be deployed through actual sales, leases, or agency contracts. This translates into severe operational and legal complexity.

    To replicate the economics of conventional finance, Islamic institutions must rely on layered, multi-contract structures involving Special Purpose Vehicles (SPVs), purchase undertakings, and the continual transfer of physical or constructive ownership. These structural requirements drastically inflate documentation costs, legal fees, and the expenses associated with Shariah advisory boards. Furthermore, the requirement to transfer asset titles can trigger significant financial burdens, such as capital gains taxes, stamp duties, and registration levies, unless a specific jurisdiction has enacted tax neutrality laws for Islamic finance. The resulting friction makes Islamic financial products inherently more expensive, administratively burdensome, and less agile than their conventional counterparts.

    Cosmetic replication fails long-term as substance scrutiny intensifies A persistent and growing critique of Islamic finance is that it prioritizes "form over substance." Products are meticulously engineered to pass the legal mechanics of Shariah contract law, yet their economic substance deliberately mimics conventional, interest-based debt. For example, Islamic lease (Ijara) profit rates are routinely pegged to conventional benchmarks like LIBOR or SOFR, effectively sheltering the bank from commercial risk.

    However, this "Shariah arbitrage" is proving unsustainable in the long term. As substance scrutiny intensifies, standard-setters are forcing structural corrections. AAOIFI has begun strictly enforcing its mandates, such as Standard 59 on the Sale of Debt and the forthcoming Standard 62 on Sukuk, which push the market away from unsecured, debt-like structures toward true asset-backed securitization with genuine risk-sharing and strict tangibility ratios.

    Furthermore, the legal fragility of cosmetic structures has been exposed in the courts. In the landmark Dana Gas case, the issuer attempted to default on its sukuk by claiming the instruments were no longer Shariah-compliant under evolving interpretations, highlighting the severe reputational and legal risks of utilizing complex, synthetic Islamic wrappers. As the Muslim middle-class and ethical investors increasingly demand authentic, socially responsible risk-sharing rather than conventional debt in an "Islamic shell," the industry's reliance on cosmetic replication risks severe reputational damage and the total dilution of its distinct ethical brand.

    Executive Refresher

    - **Core Prohibitions — Know the Three Before You Structure:** Riba (interest), Gharar (excessive uncertainty), and Maysir (gambling) are the three fundamental prohibitions that define the outer limits of any Islamic financial transaction. Before evaluating a product or executing a deal, practitioners must verify that each prohibition is unambiguously satisfied — technical workarounds that preserve form while circumventing substance will not survive rigorous Shariah scrutiny. - **Positive Requirements Are Equally Binding:** Islamic finance does not merely prohibit — it mandates asset-backing, genuine risk transfer, ethical activity screening, and real economy linkage. Every structure must demonstrate that it creates value through trade, partnership, or service rather than through the passage of time, and that the financial institution genuinely participates in the commercial risk of the transaction. - **Legal Sources and the Role of Ijtihad:** The four sources of Islamic law — Quran, Sunnah, Ijma, and Qiyas/Ijtihad — operate in a hierarchy. In modern Islamic finance, Ijtihad (independent scholarly reasoning) is the mechanism through which established principles are applied to novel instruments. The quality of this reasoning, and the credentials of the scholars performing it, directly determine the robustness of any Shariah certification. - **Substance Over Form is Non-Negotiable:** A structure that is Shariah-compliant in documentation but replicates a conventional lending relationship in economic substance will increasingly attract regulatory scrutiny, scholar criticism, and reputational risk. The trend in AAOIFI and IFSB standards has been toward closing form-over-substance loopholes — practitioners should anticipate this when advising on long-dated structures. - **Maqasid al-Shariah as the North Star:** The higher objectives of Islamic law — protecting life, intellect, wealth, lineage, and religion — provide the ethical compass beyond technical compliance. Products should be evaluated against whether they genuinely promote financial inclusion, fair dealing, and social welfare, not merely whether they pass a formal Shariah checklist.
