    Module 10: New Developments, Innovation, and Debate¶

    10.1 Form vs Substance (Still Central)¶

    The form versus substance debate is one of the most critical and contentious issues in modern Islamic finance. While the industry is fundamentally built on the prohibitions of riba (interest) and gharar (excessive uncertainty), market practices frequently prioritize the legal form of Islamic contracts over their underlying economic substance [1], [2], [3]. This results in complex financial engineering designed to replicate the risk and return profiles of conventional debt while maintaining surface-level Shariah compliance [3], [4].

    Academic Criticism of "Islamisation" and Shari'a Arbitrage Prominent academics heavily criticize this "Islamisation of conventional products." El-Gamal (2006) characterizes this practice as "Shari'a arbitrage" or rent-seeking, arguing that the industry often repackages conventional interest-based structures using premodern Arabic contract names [5], [6], [7]. This approach adds transaction costs and inefficiencies without serving the true macroeconomic or ethical objectives of Islamic law (maqasid al-Shariah) [7], [8], [9].

    Hanif (2016) highlights that while the legal forms of these contracts align with Islamic theory, their economic substance is virtually indistinguishable from conventional finance [10]. Hamour et al. (2019) describe this phenomenon as a "Substance Gap" between Sharia rulings and modern instrument structures [11]. A primary example is the industry's massive over-reliance on debt-like instruments—such as murabaha (cost-plus financing) and organized tawarruq—which scholars refer to as the "Murabaha Syndrome" [12], [13]. These contracts are frequently structured with binding purchase undertakings and fixed profit rates benchmarked to conventional indices (like LIBOR or SOFR), effectively transferring all risk to the borrower and mimicking a conventional loan [14], [15], [16], [17].

    Calls for Refocus on Community Banking and Meaningful Risk-Sharing In response to these shortcomings, academic and reformist voices advocate for redefining the "Islamic" brand around genuine social and economic development rather than contract mechanics [18], [19], [20]. This refocusing includes: * A return to true Profit-and-Loss Sharing (PLS): Scholars urge the industry to expand equity-based contracts like mudaraba and musharaka, which embody the foundational Islamic principle that profit is only justified when accompanied by risk (al-ghunm bil-ghurm) [21], [22], [23], [24]. * Embracing Mutuality: There are strong proposals to adopt mutual corporate structures—such as cooperative takaful, credit unions, and mosque-based community banking—which naturally align with Islamic ideals of risk mitigation, social justice, and financial inclusion [25], [26], [27], [20].

    Impact on Product Acceptability and Investor Confidence The persistent divergence between legal form and economic substance threatens the credibility and acceptability of Islamic financial products [28], [29], [30]. Educated Muslim consumers and conventional investors increasingly view heavily engineered products as "Shariah-washing" or legal tricks (hilah) designed to circumvent prohibitions rather than offer ethical alternatives [31], [32], [33], [34].

    A watershed moment for investor confidence occurred in 2007 when Sheikh Muhammad Taqi Usmani, chairman of the AAOIFI Shariah board, declared that up to 85% of existing sukuk were not fully Shariah-compliant [35], [36]. He noted that capital guarantees and fixed repurchase agreements insulated investors from true risk, violating core Islamic principles [36], [37]. This critique caused a severe temporary disruption in the market, driving issuance down drastically in 2008 as investors and issuers questioned the authenticity and legal safety of their holdings [35], [36]. The failure to deliver on the promised ethical substance can lead to a massive loss of trust, which is a systemic vulnerability for the entire sector [38], [30].

    Shaping Regulatory Direction To combat these concerns, international standard-setting bodies like the Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI) are increasingly steering regulatory frameworks toward strict substance compliance [39], [40]. * Standard 59: The implementation of AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 59 (Sale of Debt) recently mandated higher tangibility ratios to ensure that hybrid sukuk remain tethered to actual physical assets rather than debt receivables [41], [40]. * Standard 62: More profoundly, the proposed AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 62 seeks to re-anchor the global sukuk market to its true risk-sharing and asset-backed foundations [42], [43].

    However, regulators face a delicate policy trade-off. While immediate, strict enforcement of Standard 62 would maximize Shariah authenticity and purify the market, it risks near-term market destabilization, fragmentation, and a contraction in sukuk issuance [42], [44], [45]. Consequently, industry analysts recommend a calibrated, phased adoption of these regulatory standards, allowing legal frameworks to adapt and ensuring that religious fidelity is harmonized with market pragmatism and stability [42], [45].

    10.2 AAOIFI Standard 62 and Standardisation Pressure¶

    AAOIFI Shariah Standard No. 62 represents a fundamental shift in the regulatory landscape of Islamic finance, aiming to pull the global sukuk market away from debt-replicating structures and toward genuine risk-sharing. Released as a draft in November 2023, the standard mandates the actual legal transfer of ownership (a "true sale") of underlying assets from issuers to investors, explicitly prohibiting explicit or implicit capital guarantees like fixed-price purchase undertakings [1-3].

    This initiative has sparked intense, high-level debate across the industry, forcing stakeholders to weigh the pursuit of strict Shariah authenticity against the preservation of market stability [4, 5].

    Impact on Existing Asset-Based Sukuk Structures Historically, the vast majority of sukuk—upwards of 95%—have been structured as "asset-based" instruments [6]. In these structures, investors hold a beneficial interest in an asset, but their ultimate recourse is to the issuer's credit, effectively treating the sukuk like conventional unsecured debt [7]. To protect investors' principal, originators provide a "purchase undertaking" to buy back the assets at face value at maturity or upon default [1, 8].

    Standard 62 aims to rewrite this rulebook by mandating a transition to "asset-backed" structures, where sukuk holders must share the actual rewards and risks (al-ghunm wa al-ghurm) of the underlying assets [1]. Without par-value guarantees, investors must rely entirely on the performance and liquidation value of the assets [1, 3]. If Standard 62 is strictly enforced, most existing asset-based structures would become unviable or require significant restructuring, fundamentally altering the risk-return profile of the market [9].

    Concerns About Cost and Liquidity Impacts of True Legal Asset Transfer The requirement for a "true sale" introduces severe legal and operational frictions. Transferring legal title to an issuing Special Purpose Vehicle (SPV) often triggers significant transaction costs, including taxes, registration fees, and procedural delays [10]. These administrative burdens could increase structuring fees and make large-scale issuances highly unattractive for corporate and sovereign issuers [10].

    Furthermore, true asset transfer forces investors to accept real economic risk of loss, which deeply contradicts the capital preservation mindset dominant in fixed-income markets [11]. Market participants warn that pushing sukuk away from a conventional bond risk profile could severely weaken demand from international, non-Islamic investors [12]. This threatens to reduce secondary market liquidity, widen pricing premia, and negatively affect the funding and liquidity profiles of Islamic banks that rely on sukuk as high-quality liquid assets [12, 13]. The initial uncertainty surrounding the standard already contributed to a notable slowdown in global sukuk issuance in late 2023 [3, 14].

    Market Participant and Rating Agency Warnings About Fragmentation Rating agencies, including Fitch and S&P, have issued strong warnings that Standard 62 could fragment the global sukuk market [12, 15]. Because AAOIFI standard adoption is uneven globally—mandatory in jurisdictions like the UAE and Bahrain, but not formally adopted in Malaysia (the world's largest sukuk market)—divergent regulatory paths could create segmented markets with conflicting compliance benchmarks [12, 16-18].

    Additionally, rating agencies warn of a bifurcation in credit ratings. If sukuk repayment amounts become subject to the market risk of the underlying assets, the instruments may no longer meet the traditional definition of debt [3, 19]. Consequently, these true asset-backed sukuk could lose their conventional debt ratings, be rated on an entirely different scale, or be deemed unrateable equity, heavily deterring institutional investors who require investment-grade debt ratings [3, 4, 19].

    Possibility of Delayed or Softened Implementation In response to widespread industry pushback over the contractual and operational complexities of the draft, the possibility of a delayed and softened implementation has become a reality [18]. In April 2025, AAOIFI confirmed that the draft standard was undergoing revisions based on industry feedback, with the official launch and implementation pushed back to 2026 [18].

    This delay has eased immediate market panic, offering hope that the final version will incorporate more pragmatic provisions [18]. Regulators are expected to pursue a calibrated sequencing of the standard, phasing in tangibility and true-sale thresholds over several years [4, 20]. To prevent a market freeze, jurisdictions may also introduce supportive legal reforms—such as tax neutrality for asset transfers and the establishment of collateral registries—while allowing targeted carve-outs for sovereign issuers, ultimately seeking to balance religious fidelity with financial viability [4, 20-22].

    10.3 Digitisation and Tokenisation¶

    The digitisation and tokenisation of Islamic finance are fundamentally transforming the structuring, accessibility, and cost-efficiency of the market. By leveraging blockchain technology and smart contracts, the industry is addressing historical barriers to entry and reshaping how Shariah-compliant assets are distributed.

    Cost Reductions and Fractionalisation Historically, traditional sukuk issuance has been a highly expensive process, costing between USD 500,000 and USD 1.5 million in legal and banking fees [1, 2]. This high barrier to entry largely restricted the market to sovereign entities and large corporations. However, blockchain technology is dramatically reducing these costs by automating legal, accounting, and payment workflows [2, 3]. Digital platforms such as Wethaq and ADIB’s Smart Sukuk are utilizing blockchain to fractionalise asset ownership, enabling SMEs to issue "micro-sukuk" for as little as USD 10,000 [1, 2]. This fractionalisation democratizes the market, providing smaller enterprises with vital capital while offering retail investors access to asset-backed Shariah-compliant securities [4].

    Regulatory Initiatives: Malaysia SC and Khazanah Collaboration Regulators are actively exploring digital infrastructure to future-proof the Islamic capital market. The Securities Commission (SC) Malaysia has partnered with Khazanah Nasional to explore the issuance of a tokenised bond or sukuk [2, 5, 6]. This collaborative pilot is designed to test the real-world utility of tokenisation—ranging from custodial arrangements to the deployment of smart contracts—allowing policymakers to deeply understand both the benefits and risks of integrating blockchain technology into mainstream capital markets [6].

    The 5-10 Year Outlook Over the next 5 to 10 years, the Islamic finance industry is expected to evolve significantly, driven by genuine technological innovations rather than merely synthetic product repackaging [7, 8]. Key pillars of this outlook include: * Greater Tokenisation and Retail Access: The tokenisation of real-world assets will continue to expand, converting physical properties, commodities, and business ventures into fractional digital tokens [9, 10]. This will lower investment minimums, boost secondary market liquidity, and vastly broaden retail access to Islamic capital markets globally [7, 10]. * Cross-Border Platforms: Distributed ledger technology will facilitate the growth of cross-border platforms, removing geographical friction and harmonizing Islamic finance across diverse regulatory environments. This will allow for seamless capital transfers and integrate regional hubs into a more unified global Islamic digital economy [7, 11, 12]. * Smart-Contract Automation of Profit Distribution: Viewed as a highly genuine innovation, smart contracts will automate complex Shariah-compliant agreements like Mudarabah (profit-sharing) and Musharakah (joint ventures) [7, 13]. By embedding business rules directly into the blockchain, smart contracts can automatically calculate and distribute profits based on pre-agreed ratios without the need for manual bank intervention [4, 14, 15]. This ensures absolute transparency, eliminates human error, prevents unilateral data manipulation, and completely removes the ambiguity that historically led to Shariah disputes [4, 14, 16].

    10.4 ESG and Sustainable Sukuk Convergence¶

    Natural Convergence with Islamic Finance Principles The integration of Environmental, Social, and Governance (ESG) criteria with Islamic finance represents a profound natural synergy, as both systems emphasize ethical conduct, social responsibility, and tangible impact [1-3]. Islamic finance is anchored in the Maqasid Al-Shariah (the objectives of Islamic law), which prioritize the protection of life, wealth, intellect, and the environment, aligning seamlessly with modern ESG frameworks [4, 5]. This convergence rests on several core pillars: * Ethical Screening: Both frameworks utilize negative screening to exclude investments in harmful activities, such as weapons, gambling, alcohol, and fossil fuels, while directing capital toward projects that generate measurable environmental and social benefits (maslahah) [6-9]. * Real-Economy Linkage: Islamic finance requires that transactions be backed by tangible assets and productive economic activities rather than speculative, interest-based debt (riba) [10-13]. This structural requirement ensures that funds are directly deployed into climate-resilient infrastructure, renewable energy, and green buildings [14-16]. * Transparency and Stewardship: Both systems demand robust governance, accountability, and impact reporting [7, 17]. Islamic principles view humans as trustees (khalifah) of the Earth, embedding environmental stewardship directly into the religious and financial mandate [18, 19].

    ICMA/IsDB/LSEG Guidance To standardize this rapidly expanding market, the International Capital Market Association (ICMA), the Islamic Development Bank (IsDB), and the London Stock Exchange Group (LSEG) published joint guidance in April 2024 [20, 21]. This landmark framework provides practical direction on structuring and labelling green, social, and sustainability sukuk to ensure they align with both Shariah mandates and global ICMA Principles, such as the Green Bond Principles (GBP) and Sustainability Bond Guidelines (SBG) [22-25]. By bridging Islamic structuring with international sustainable finance conventions, the guidance aims to demystify the asset class for conventional and agnostic ESG investors, enhance market transparency, and mitigate the dual risks of "greenwashing" and "Shariah-washing" [26-29].

    Green Sukuk Exceeding USD 15 Billion in 2024 Driven by regulatory clarity and rising global demand, the sustainable sukuk market has achieved record-breaking milestones. In 2024, global issuances of ESG and green sukuk grew to exceed USD 15 billion, specifically reaching approximately USD 15.4 billion (a 14.7% year-on-year increase) [6, 30-32]. This surge pushed the total outstanding value of the ESG sukuk market past the USD 50 billion mark by the end of 2024 [31-34]. Market growth was heavily propelled by corporate and financial institutions in the Gulf Cooperation Council (GCC)—particularly Saudi Arabia and the UAE—as regional governments aggressively issue debt to finance their energy transition and economic diversification agendas ahead of global climate deadlines [31, 34-36].

    Malaysia's SRI Sukuk Leadership Malaysia remains the undisputed pioneer and global laboratory for sustainable Islamic finance [37-39]. The country laid the groundwork by introducing its Sustainable and Responsible Investment (SRI) Sukuk Framework in 2014, leading to the issuance of the world's very first green sukuk by Tadau Energy in 2017 to fund a solar power plant [15, 40-42].

    Malaysia's leadership is sustained by a highly supportive regulatory ecosystem spearheaded by the Securities Commission Malaysia and Bank Negara Malaysia, which provide explicit guidelines for SRI sukuk, ASEAN sustainability-linked sukuk, and Islamic funds with waqf (endowment) features [30, 43-46]. Consequently, Malaysia saw SRI sukuk issuances soar to RM 11.9 billion in 2024, an almost ninefold increase since the market's inception [47, 48]. By integrating "Value-Based Intermediation" (VBI) into its core banking practices, Malaysia has successfully transformed green and sustainability sukuk from a niche product into a mainstream instrument for inclusive, sustainable economic growth [41, 49, 50].

    10.5 AI-Driven Shariah Compliance¶

    Artificial Intelligence (AI) is fundamentally transforming Shariah compliance in Islamic finance by shifting the industry from retrospective reviews to proactive, dynamic oversight [1, 2].

    Real-Time Transaction Monitoring Replacing Annual Audits Historically, Islamic financial institutions have relied on an annual Shariah audit to verify that operations adhered to Islamic jurisprudence [1]. Today, scholars and financial institutions are beginning to use AI to monitor transaction flows in real-time [1, 2]. This technological evolution replaces the traditional "Annual Shariah Audit" with "Instantaneous Compliance Monitoring," ensuring that financial operations are continuously tracked and validated [1, 2]. As part of this shift, online platforms are now capable of offering real-time compliance audits for Islamic investment funds [3].

    Key AI Applications in Shariah Compliance The integration of AI spans several critical compliance and operational functions: * Automated Screening: AI-powered screening tools and big data analytics are being widely deployed to enhance Shariah screening processes [3, 4]. Halal investment platforms utilize AI-driven compliance checks to automatically filter out and prevent investments in non-permissible (haram) industries, such as gambling and alcohol [5]. * Real-Time Revenue Purification: AI applications are streamlining the complex process of revenue purification by automating calculations in real-time, ensuring that any tainted income is swiftly identified for charitable donation [2]. * Document Review: AI is being utilized to automate the review of financial documents and digitalize the halal reporting process, saving time and reducing human error [2, 6]. * Advisory and Risk Evaluation: AI-driven tools are powering robo-advisory platforms to provide automated, personalized investment advice that strictly adheres to Islamic guidelines [7]. AI is also deployed to assess creditworthiness, evaluate ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) factors, and automate complex Zakat calculations using real-time financial data [8-10].

    Significant Potential for Reducing Operational Risk The move toward instantaneous, AI-driven compliance monitoring has drastically reduced operational risks for Islamic banks [1, 2]. By automating oversight, institutions can catch potential Shariah breaches the moment they occur rather than discovering them during an annual review [1, 2].

    However, the rapid deployment of AI also introduces new considerations for Islamic financial institutions. While AI provides massive efficiency gains, it requires strong governance, risk management frameworks, and continuous oversight [10]. The opaque nature of some AI models can pose challenges, particularly because algorithms may lack the nuanced interpretative judgment needed for complex Shariah rulings and differing jurisprudential interpretations across regions [10]. Consequently, Shariah boards and regulatory authorities must remain vigilant to mitigate emerging risks related to algorithmic bias, data quality, and model integrity [10].

    10.6 The Authenticity Movement¶

    The authenticity movement in Islamic finance is driving a structural shift away from "synthetic" compliance toward genuine adherence to Islamic economic principles, challenging the industry to move beyond mere legalistic forms [1-3].

    Growing Backlash Against Synthetic Products (Tawarruq) There is a growing scholarly and consumer backlash against "synthetic" products like Tawarruq (organized commodity Murabaha) [1, 2]. Tawarruq involves a multi-step transaction where a financier buys a commodity, sells it to a client on a deferred payment basis with a markup, and then the client (or an agent) immediately sells the commodity to a third party for cash [4-8]. While this fulfills the legal mechanics of a trade, critics argue it is a legal stratagem (hilah) lacking economic substance, as its sole purpose is to replicate a conventional interest-bearing cash loan [9-18]. Many scholars, including the OIC Fiqh Academy, have condemned organized Tawarruq as a deceptive practice that violates the spirit of Shariah by generating debt without promoting real, productive economic activity [19-25].

    The Future of Authentic Risk-Sharing (Musharaka) In response to these criticisms, advocates assert that the future of Islamic finance belongs to "authentic" risk-sharing models, most notably Musharaka [1, 2]. Musharaka is a joint venture partnership where all parties contribute capital and share in the actual profits based on pre-agreed ratios, while financial losses are shared strictly in proportion to their respective capital contributions [26-32]. By linking financial returns directly to the performance of underlying assets and enterprise risk, Musharaka aligns the interests of capital providers and entrepreneurs, avoiding the guaranteed fixed returns of debt-based instruments [33-37]. Because it embodies true profit-and-loss sharing (PLS) and requires genuine asset-backing, it is widely regarded as the "truest" and most authentic form of Islamic finance [38, 39].

    Utility-Based Fintech for SMEs and the Unbanked The authenticity movement also champions "utility-based" fintech products that solve real economic problems for SMEs and the unbanked [1, 2]. Innovative Islamic fintech solutions—such as Shariah-compliant equity crowdfunding, peer-to-peer (P2P) lending, and blockchain-based tokenization (micro-sukuk)—democratize access to capital by removing traditional intermediaries and directly connecting investors to real-world projects [40-47]. For example, equity-based crowdfunding platforms allow retail investors to fund SMEs on a profit-sharing basis, helping to bridge the massive global SME financing gap [48-51]. These digital, utility-driven models fulfill the broader social justice and wealth-circulation objectives (Maqasid al-Shariah) of Islamic finance by prioritizing financial inclusion and community welfare [46, 52-56].

    Influence on Academia, Product Design, and Marketing This push for authenticity is strongest in academic circles and among next-generation scholars, but it is actively influencing product design and marketing [2]. Academics have long criticized the "Islamization" of conventional products, calling for a refocus on community banking, socially responsible investing (SRI), and genuine risk-sharing [57-62]. Consequently, financial institutions and fintechs are adapting their strategies to develop authentic private credit platforms, venture capital structures, and ESG-aligned sustainable funds [63-68]. By focusing on ethical investment and substantive Shariah compliance rather than mere legal labels, these products are successfully appealing to both faith-conscious Muslims and a broader base of values-driven global investors [69-73].

    10.7 Private Market Innovation¶

    Private markets represent the "priority frontier" for Islamic finance, providing a natural fit for the industry’s core principles of risk-sharing, asset linkage, and real-economy financing [1-3]. As the industry matures, significant innovation is occurring across private market segments to bridge the gap between conventional Western capital structures and Shariah compliance [4, 5].

    Here is an in-depth look at the emerging innovations in Islamic private markets:

    1. Shariah-Compliant Private Credit and DIFC-Based Innovations Conventional private credit is fundamentally based on interest-bearing debt, which violates the Islamic prohibition of riba (interest) [6, 7]. To capture the massive demand from European and Middle Eastern family offices seeking 7–10% non-correlated yields, the Islamic finance industry has engineered sophisticated private credit alternatives [8]. * Commodity Murabaha Structuring: Islamic private credit delivers financing using sale, lease, and agency structures—specifically Murabaha, Ijara, and Wakala—while maintaining enforceable security and creditor protections [6, 7]. The recent PwC/DIFC private credit market report acknowledges that Commodity Murabaha (Tawarruq) has become a standard structuring tool for DIFC-based private credit funds. This structure is highly favored because it is operationally standardized and efficiently replicates the drawdown and amortizing profiles of conventional credit facilities [5, 9-11]. * Digital Onboarding and Evergreen Platforms: New product formats are transforming distribution. For example, the Emirates NBD Shari'ah Private Financing Fund (EmFIN) operates as an evergreen, continuously offered platform with digital onboarding [5, 12, 13]. It utilizes Wakala (agency) layers to wrap conventional private credit exposures into a Shariah-compliant format, granting local retail and institutional investors seamless access to global private market deal flow [14, 15].

    2. Tokenised Real Assets Because Islamic finance strictly mandates that investments be backed by tangible assets, the tokenization of real-world assets is an ideal technological evolution for the industry [16]. * Fractionalizing Real Estate and Sukuk: Blockchain enables the fractional ownership of properties, commodities, and Sukuk (Islamic certificates). "Tokenized real estate Musharaka" is emerging as a way to create highly liquid secondary markets for prime real estate in hubs like London, Dubai, and Riyadh [17]. * Micro-Sukuk via Smart Contracts: Digital platforms like Wethaq and ADIB’s Smart Sukuk use distributed ledger technology to fractionalize ownership, enabling SMEs to issue "micro-sukuk" for as little as US$10,000 [18, 19]. Smart contracts automate the execution of Shariah rules, managing real-time profit distributions and ensuring that returns are directly tied to the performance of the underlying physical assets without the need for costly intermediaries [20, 21]. Tokenised real assets are now widely viewed as the frontier of Islamic capital markets [5, 9].

    3. Shariah-Compliant Venture Capital (VC) Venture capital is theoretically the "truest" form of Islamic finance because it relies on profit-and-loss sharing (PLS) and equity creation rather than debt [2, 22]. The conventional GP/LP (General Partner/Limited Partner) fund structure naturally mirrors the classical Islamic Mudaraba contract, where LPs act as capital providers (Rab-al-Maal) and GPs act as managers (Mudarib) earning a management fee and a share of profits (carried interest) [2, 23-25]. * Overcoming Structural Frictions: Conventional VC relies heavily on interest-bearing convertible notes, venture debt, and preference shares with guaranteed liquidation preferences, all of which clash with Shariah law [26-30]. * Innovative Workarounds: To remain commercially competitive, Islamic VC funds are replacing preference-share certainty with partnership-consistent, tiered profit-sharing agreements under Musharaka [9, 27]. For early-stage seed funding, Islamic VCs are adopting Simple Agreements for Future Equity (SAFE) and Advanced Subscription Agreements (ASAs), which commit to future equity ownership without an interim interest-bearing debt phase [31].

    4. Cross-Border Co-Investment Platforms as the Frontier To fulfill the demand for direct deal access and cross-border capital mobility, Shariah-compliant co-investment platforms have emerged as a massive "white space" opportunity [5, 9, 32]. * Family Office Platforms: These platforms allow Ultra-High-Net-Worth (UHNW) families and institutional investors to bypass traditional pooled funds and access direct deal flow. They typically utilize Diminishing Musharaka (where the entrepreneur or sponsor gradually buys out the investor's equity stake) or Wakala structures [32, 33]. * Acquisition Finance: These cross-border platforms act as a bridge between cash-rich GCC capital and Western mid-market M&A targets. By using "Islamic leverage" structures—such as mezzanine Murabaha facilities combined with Wa'd (unilateral promises) to simulate equity kickers or warrants—these platforms allow Islamic investors to execute complex buyouts globally without violating prohibitions against conventional interest-bearing leverage [17, 34, 35].

    Knowledge Check Quiz

    1. What is AAOIFI Standard 62 and why is it contentious?
    Show answer Standard 62 is a proposed sukuk standard that would require more consistent legal transfer of assets to sukuk investors. It is contentious because if broadly applied, many asset-based sukuk (the dominant market structure) would require restructuring — raising costs and potentially reducing market liquidity.
    2. How does tokenisation reduce barriers to sukuk issuance?
    Show answer Traditional sukuk issuance costs USD 500K–1.5M in legal, structuring, and listing fees. Digital platforms using blockchain-based fractional sukuk certificates can issue smaller tranches from USD 10,000, dramatically lowering the minimum ticket size and expanding retail and smaller issuer access.
